# RP04. Demand-Response Intervention Typologies and the Empirical Field Efficacy of Peer-Comparison Messaging

## Section A. Direct answer

A statement that field evidence shows peer-comparison messaging has "limited efficacy" is **factually unfair and inaccurate if applied to residential energy conservation broadly**, but is a **defensible and scientifically accurate reading if applied specifically to dynamic peak demand-response (DR) events**. In the domain of general electricity conservation across billing cycles, peer comparison delivered through Home Energy Reports (HERs) is one of the most rigorously evidenced and replicated non-price interventions in economics, consistently yielding a **1.4% to 2.0% average reduction in electricity demand across millions of households** in over 111 randomized controlled trials (Allcott 2011, 2015), rising to **6.3% in the top pre-treatment consumption decile**. However, in the domain of **within-day critical peak demand response (kW reduction during 1–4 hour grid events)**, peer comparison and moral suasion exhibit **rapid decay (often vanishing by the 3rd or 4th consecutive event)** and achieve vastly smaller, less reliable peak-load reductions than dynamic pricing (10% to 17% sustained reduction) or automated Direct Load Control (20% to 50% appliance load shed) (Ito et al. 2018). Furthermore, the frequently cited building-energy sources for this three-way typology—Gyamfi & Krumdieck (2011) and Albadi & El-Saadany (2008)—contain **zero empirical findings on peer-comparison or social-norm messaging efficacy**, making their citation for social-norm efficacy an outright citation defect.

Do not dismiss peer comparison as having "limited efficacy" in general energy conservation.

Treat occupant responsiveness across demographics as an empirical distribution conditioned on baseline discretionary appliance stock and thermal comfort bounds, rather than speculative behavioral archetypes.
* [Tied to B14, B21, B22] **Contextualise European modeling targets (Spain, UK, Italy)**: When applying DR or behavioral parameters to European archetypes, scale baseline responsive loads downward relative to US benchmarks. Acknowledge that lower residential electrification (gas heating/water) and lower AC penetration in the UK and Southern Europe constrain total peak-shedding potential to 1–3% under default TOU tariffs and ~0.7% under social norm interventions.

## Section G. Contradictions, gaps, open questions, and your own negative controls

### Contradictions and Gaps in the Literature
* **Energy Conservation (kWh) vs Peak Shaving (kW) Conflation**: The single largest source of contradiction in building-energy papers is the conflation of *total electricity volume conservation* (measured over monthly billing cycles) with *peak demand response* (measured over 1 to 4 hour critical stress events). Authors citing Allcott (2011) to justify peak load shedding in building simulation are misapplying a 2.0% chronic conservation effect to short-duration peak flexibility.
* **Gyamfi & Krumdieck (2011) and Albadi & El-Saadany (2008) Citation Drift**: Numerous building simulation and multi-agent modeling papers cite Gyamfi & Krumdieck (2011) alongside Albadi & El-Saadany (2008) as joint authority for a tripartite "regulatory / economic / social" residential DR framework. Reading the original texts reveals that Albadi & El-Saadany is an engineering review of wholesale/retail electricity market programs with zero behavioral content, while Gyamfi & Krumdieck is a 194-household stated-preference survey in Christchurch testing price, carbon, and blackout warnings—neither paper contains any descriptive peer comparison or Home Energy Report trial.
* **US vs European Replication Gap**: While early US literature celebrated HERs as universally cost-effective, rigorous European field experiments (e.g. Andor et al. 2020 in Germany) show that lower baseline household consumption and higher fixed delivery costs make printed peer-comparison mailings economically unviable in Europe unless combined with digital/smart-meter channels.
