# RL29. Non-Convex and Courtyard Footprint Subdivision in UBEM: Methods, Fallback Bias, Selection Denominators, and Geometric Robustness

## Section A. Direct answer

The energy modeling literature demonstrates that the one-zone-per-floor fallback is systematically biased rather than merely noisy, under-predicting annual space heating demand by 5% to 15% and under-predicting peak heating power by 10% to 25% in heating-dominated climates due to instantaneous spatial aggregation of non-coincident solar gains, internal loads, and occupant diversity. Consequently, fallback buildings cannot be pooled with dwelling-partitioned buildings in a single statistical denominator without introducing severe confounding bias. For non-convex, courtyard, and deep floor plates, the published literature employs straight-skeleton wavefront propagation (e.g. in Dragonfly and AutoZoner) and exact convex polygon partitioning (e.g. Hertel-Mehlhorn decomposition and Convex Partition Zoner) to generate dual-aspect courtyard wings, perimeter zones (4.5 to 6.0 m depth), and unconditioned or service core buffers. However, published UBEM workflows almost universally evaluate these geometric algorithms on idealized or synthetic geometries; when deployed on raw cadastral data, 10% to 40% of buildings fail geometric validity unless an automated pre-cleaning pipeline is applied. Ramer-Douglas-Peucker polygon simplification with a perpendicular distance tolerance of 0.10 m to 0.25 m is standard UBEM practice, safely eliminating vertex bloat (such as 173-vertex cadastral rings exceeding EnergyPlus input limits) while inducing less than 0.5% error in annual heating demand and less than 1.0% in peak load. Finally, planar partition area audits in projected coordinate reference systems must evaluate dimensionless relative area tolerances (1e-7 to 1e-6) on local centroid-translated coordinates rather than absolute square-meter thresholds (1e-8 m2), which fail due to double-precision floating-point cancellation at standard geographic coordinate magnitudes.

## Section G. Contradictions, gaps, open questions, and negative controls

### Contradictions and Gaps
* **Idealized Algorithms vs Real Cadastral Noise**: While the computational geometry literature asserts that straight-skeleton and convex decomposition algorithms are mathematically complete, UBEM implementations frequently encounter degenerate topology on real municipal cadastral data (such as self-intersecting boundaries, collinear edges, and sub-centimeter vertex spikes). Academic papers rarely report these failure rates, creating an apparent contradiction between theoretical algorithmic availability and practical implementation yield. This gap is resolved by incorporating mandatory topological pre-cleaning (snapping and RDP simplification) before partitioning.
* **Zoning Resolution vs Computational Tractability**: Architectural simulation literature advocates room-by-room or dwelling-by-dwelling multi-zone models to capture thermal comfort and peak loads, whereas district-scale UBEM literature frequently justifies single-zone-per-floor models on grounds of computational scaling. Our findings reconcile this: while single-zone models remain necessary for computational scalability on full city stocks, they cannot be mixed with multi-zone models in statistical comparisons due to systematic negative load bias.
