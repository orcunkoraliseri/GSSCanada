# **National Time-Use Surveys for Building Occupancy Profiling in Energy Simulations: A Methodological Assessment**

Occupant behavior and spatial presence represent primary drivers of uncertainty in building energy simulation (BES).1 Historically, energy models have relied on static, deterministic schedules—such as those published in early building standards—which fail to capture the stochastic diversity and temporal variations of real-world human behavior, leading to discrepancies of up to 40% in peak capacity estimates.3 To resolve this, microdata from national time-use surveys (TUS) such as the American Time Use Survey (ATUS) 5, the Harmonised European Time Use Survey (HETUS) 7, the United Kingdom Time Use Survey (UKTUS) 9, and the Canadian General Social Survey on Time Use (GSS TUS) 11 are increasingly employed to construct empirical, probabilistically derived occupancy profiles.1 This report evaluates the methodologies, structural coding schemes, limitations, and supplementary integration strategies required to successfully utilize TUS data for building-occupancy scheduling across diverse building use types.

The primary location-coding systems differ across national jurisdictions, presenting distinct classification structures and levels of longitudinal stability:

### **American Time Use Survey (ATUS)**

The ATUS utilizes a four-digit location classification system.20 Code 0101 represents "Respondent's home or yard" (indicating residential presence), code 0102 denotes "Respondent's workplace" (commercial office or institutional presence), and codes 0106 ("Grocery store") and 0107 ("Other store, mall") indicate presence in retail spaces.20 The ATUS location scheme is highly stable, remaining largely unchanged since its inception in 2003, with minor additions such as separate codes for banks (0111), gyms (0112), and post offices (0113) introduced in 2004\.20 However, location data are not collected for highly personal activities like sleeping (010101) or grooming (010201), which are designated as "out of universe" and assigned a default value of \-1.18 Analysts must logically impute these \-1 episodes as "at home" (0101).18

### **Harmonised European Time Use Survey (HETUS)**

The HETUS guidelines establish a standardized two-digit location classification.24 Code 11 represents "Home", 12 represents "Weekend home or holiday apartment", 13 designates "Workplace or school", 15 represents "Restaurant, cafe or pub", 16 represents "Shopping centres, markets, other shops", and 17 represents "Hotel, guesthouse, camping site".24 This scheme has exhibited exceptional stability across the 2000, 2008, and 2018 survey waves, ensuring long-term comparability across participating European nations.7

### **Canadian General Social Survey (GSS)**

The Canadian GSS location codes have evolved from basic categories in early cycles to a structured three-digit system.11 In the 2015 and 2022 cycles, key codes include 300 ("At home or on property"), 301 ("At place of work or school"), 306 ("Grocery store, other stores or mall"), 309 ("Restaurant, bar or club"), and 311 ("Medical, dental or other health clinic").27 While these codes provide a high level of detail for mapping commercial uses, methodological redesigns in Canada have introduced structural breaks that complicate longitudinal processing.28

## **Limitations of Time-Use Surveys in Non-Residential Environments**

While TUS data provides robust empirical foundations for residential occupancy, its application to non-residential and commercial building energy models is constrained by several fundamental survey-methodology limitations:

### **Household-Centric Sampling Frames**

The target populations of national time-use surveys are restricted to resident members of private households.5 Because the sampling unit is the household rather than the commercial property, non-residential buildings—such as offices, retail spaces, and restaurants—are observed only from the "visitor" or "worker" side.33 Consequently, TUS data cannot provide a direct, closed-system measure of the total simultaneous occupant load inside any specific non-residential building.33 The aggregate density curve must instead be synthesized from individual, unlinked personal trajectories across the broader urban environment.4

### **The Tourist and Transient Population Blind Spot**

TUS sampling designs explicitly exclude institutionalized populations, non-residents, and out-of-region tourists.7 This creates a severe under-coverage bias for hospitality and travel-related use types.7 For example, the occupancy profile of a hotel is driven primarily by tourists, business travelers, and international visitors who are entirely out of frame in national surveys.7 Relying solely on TUS data to model hotel energy loads results in an incomplete representation of occupant density.7

### **Diary Mechanics, Over-Sampling, and Recall Errors**

The structural mechanics of time diaries introduce several systematic biases:

* **Single-Day Snapshots:** Most TUS protocols (such as ATUS and GSS) collect a retrospective diary covering only a single 24-hour period per respondent.5 This cross-sectional design cannot capture day-to-day behavioral correlations within the same household, which is a major limitation for simulating multi-day demand profiles.39  
* **Temporal Imbalances:** Weekend days are heavily oversampled (e.g., ATUS allocates 10% to each weekday and 25% to Saturday and Sunday, respectively) to generate statistically stable weekend profiles.5 This imbalance requires the application of complex, design-specific survey weights to prevent weekdays from being statistically overshadowed in long-term energy simulations.5  
* **Cognitive and Recall Errors:** Respondents tend to round activity durations to 15-, 30-, or 60-minute boundaries (telescoping), which smooths out sharp peaks and transitions in occupancy.41 Short-duration occupancy events—such as brief shopping trips or work breaks—are frequently omitted due to respondent fatigue.40

### **COVID-19 and Post-Pandemic Collection Shocks**

The COVID-19 pandemic introduced both operational and behavioral disruptions to TUS datasets:

* **Collection Mode Shifts:** To adapt to social distancing, agencies transitioned rapidly from telephone-assisted (CATI) formats to self-administered electronic questionnaires (EQ).11 These multi-mode collection sequences introduced operational breaks, impacting response rates and altering how respondents classified complex simultaneous activities.28  
* **Structural Behavioral Changes:** The pandemic accelerated the adoption of remote and hybrid working arrangements.43 Post-pandemic data (such as the 2022 Canadian GSS and the 2023 UKTUS) reveal fragmented working profiles, with distinct "homeworker", "commuter", and "hybrid worker" lifestyles.43 Energy models utilizing pre-2020 TUS profiles will significantly overestimate commercial office occupancy and underestimate residential base loads during typical business hours.43

## **Published Precedents and Modeling Approaches**

Several researchers have developed methodologies to translate categorical TUS diary entries into quantitative schedules suitable for BES tools:

### **Richardson et al. (CREST Occupancy Model)**

The landmark CREST model utilized the 2000 UK Time Use Survey to construct an inhomogeneous, first-order Markov chain model to simulate active and inactive residential occupancy.13 The states of the Markov chain represent whether an occupant is present and active (and thus generating internal heat gains and using appliances) or asleep/absent.13 The state transition probabilities are time-dependent, calculating the probability of an occupant changing states at each 10-minute interval based on household size and day of the week.13

* *Stated Caveat:* The model relies on historical time-use distributions that cannot adapt dynamically to sudden external disruptions or local weather anomalies.13

### **Sood et al. (Zone-Wise and Room-Level Models)**

Building upon the Markovian framework, Sood et al. utilized the UKTUS 2014-15 dataset to extract 5,376 highly granular, day-wise room-level occupancy profiles.14 By combining activity diaries, demographic metadata (household size and age), dwelling characteristics, and seasonal factors, the researchers generated realistic schedules for multi-scale residential building archetypes.14 In cases where room-level locations were not explicitly recorded, the researchers developed a Hidden Markov Model (HMM) to statistically infer unobserved room states (e.g., kitchen, living room, bedroom) based on the recorded activity sequences.13

* *Stated Caveat:* Raw TUS data is highly qualitative and categorical, making it fundamentally incompatible with BES formats without extensive preprocessing, data alignment, and logical imputation of missing attributes.14

### **Banfi et al. (Urban-Scale Stochastic Modeling)**

Focusing on bridging the gap between residential and commercial sectors, Banfi et al. analyzed the 2013 Italian Time Use Survey to construct urban-scale occupant movement models.4 Using cluster analysis, the authors identified 18 daily movement patterns (lifestyles) distinct for weekdays, Saturdays, and Sundays.4 This allowed them to scale individual time-use trajectories across diverse building types in an urban district rather than treating buildings in isolation.4

* *Stated Caveat:* Standard, static building energy schedules (e.g., ASHRAE 90.1 or European ECB benchmarks) consistently underestimate the real-world variability and diversity of human schedules.3 However, translating individual-level trajectories into commercial building loads requires assumptions about building assignment, as TUS datasets do not record the physical location of the commercial properties.4

## **Canadian GSS Time Use Specifics (Cycles 2005–2022)**

The Canadian GSS Time Use program, conducted by Statistics Canada, provides a valuable resource for Canadian BES, but its longitudinal application is complicated by major survey redesigns across its key cycles 11:

### **The 2005 (Cycle 19\) and 2010 (Cycle 24\) Cycles**

These waves featured highly detailed diaries collected via Computer-Assisted Telephone Interviewing (CATI).28 The 2010 cycle represented the peak of descriptive granularity, utilizing 264 distinct activity codes and incorporating simultaneous activities for the first time.27 The location component in 2005 was modified specifically to align with the US ATUS "where were you" component to enable cross-border comparisons.50 Location questions utilized a basic categorical structure (e.g., 1 Respondent's home, 2 Workplace, 3 Someone else's home, 4 Restaurant/bar, 5 Place of worship).51

### **The 2015 (Cycle 29\) Redesign and Reductions**

In 2015, Statistics Canada introduced a redesigned methodology to reduce respondent burden and lower operational costs 27:

* **The "Light Diary" Shift:** The highly detailed activity classification crashed from 264 codes in 2010 to just 64 codes in 2015\.27 This represented a significant reduction in the precision of documented activities.27  
* **Collection Mode Evolution:** Interviews transitioned from purely telephone-based CATI to a multi-mode approach, incorporating self-administered electronic questionnaires (EQ).28  
* **Workplace and Retail Coding Breaks:** The massive reduction in activity codes in 2015 represents a major coding break.28 Differentiating sub-categories of labor or commercial visits based on activity codes became impossible.27 Analysts are forced to rely heavily on the 2015 location codes (such as 301 for work/school, 306 for retail, and 309 for dining) to establish spatial presence, as the activity codes themselves are too highly aggregated to distinguish between commercial use types.27

### **The 2022 (Cycle 34/36) Transition to the GSSP**

In 2022, the survey was integrated into the new General Social Statistics Program (GSSP).11 Key changes include:

* **Dwelling-Based Frame:** The historical telephone-based random-digit-dialing (RDD) frame was completely replaced by a dwelling-based frame utilizing the Dwelling Universe File (DUF).11  
* **Administrative and Model Data Integration:** The GSSP framework integrates direct survey data with administrative records and statistical modeling to compensate for declining response rates.35  
* **Dominant Online Collection:** The majority of respondents completed their diaries online.11 This mode shift, coupled with post-COVID hybrid work habits, creates a strong discontinuity when comparing 2022 data directly with pre-pandemic cycles.11

## **Recommended Corrections and Integration Framework**

To address the inherent limitations of time-use data in commercial energy modeling, TUS profiles must be strategically paired with supplementary sources.3 The following mapping table defines the evidence quality of TUS across diverse building use types and outlines recommended supplementary integration sources:

| Building Use Type | TUS Evidence Quality | Primary Vulnerabilities / Gaps | Recommended Supplementary Source | Integration Strategy |
| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| **Single-Family Residential** | **High** 14 | Minimal, minor self-report recall error and temporal rounding.40 | Smart meter data (electricity/gas logs).52 | Calibrate TUS transition probabilities by matching occupant-related active states with smart meter load curve signatures.52 |
| **Multi-Family Residential** | **High** 14 | Shared space occupancy and common area loads are not captured.13 | Sub-metered common area energy data, occupancy sensors.52 | Superimpose baseline common-area occupancy curves on top of the individual household TUS profiles.54 |
| **Commercial Office (Corporate/Single-Tenant)** | **Moderate** 3 | Fails to capture hybrid telework schedules, localized absenteeism, and corporate holiday patterns.43 | Badge access control systems, Wi-Fi positioning logs.33 | Scale TUS-derived presence curves using real-time employee badge-swipe data and local remote-work ratios.33 |
| **Commercial Office (Multi-Tenant/Public)** | **Moderate to Low** 3 | Misses external contractors, clients, and irregular maintenance visitors.33 | CO2 and environmental sensors, Wi-Fi positioning.33 | Use physical CO2 mass-balance models to adjust worker counts and infer visitor load variations.55 |
| **Retail (Grocery & Mercantile)** | **Low** 3 | Highly sensitive to temporal rounding; misses tourist footfall and impulsivity.3 | Google Maps popular times API, footfall counters.3 | Calibrate TUS shopping durations by scaling the profile shape to match local mobile location-based popular times curves.3 |
| **Restaurants & Food Service** | **Low** 3 | Ignores out-of-region tourist dining; fails to capture food preparation setup times.7 | Commercial reservation transaction logs, Google popular times.3 | Align customer occupancy peaks using Google times and adjust kitchen worker schedules using national labor shift statistics.3 |
| **Hotels & Lodging** | **Insufficient** 7 | Out of frame: tourists, business travelers, and non-residents are excluded from TUS.7 | National tourism statistics, local hotel occupancy registers.7 | Substitute TUS entirely. Derive guest profiles from tourism occupancy rates and back-of-house staff profiles from corporate rosters. |
| **Schools & Universities** | **Moderate to Low** 33 | Student commuter trajectories are complex; fails to resolve classroom-level transitions.39 | Class enrollment databases, campus Wi-Fi density logs.33 | Apply campus Wi-Fi density curves to distribute TUS-derived school presence into specific academic zones.33 |
| **Hospitals & Healthcare Clinics** | **Insufficient** 36 | Institutionalized populations and inpatients are excluded from the sampling frame.35 | Hospital administrative data, clinical shift rosters.36 | Substitute TUS entirely. Utilize inpatient bed-turnover logs and clinical shift rosters to reconstruct schedules. |

### **Building Use Types where TUS is Insufficient**

Based on the mapping analysis, national time-use surveys are structurally insufficient and **must be replaced or augmented** with supplementary datasets for the following building use types:

1. **Hotels, Motels, and Lodging Facilities:** The complete exclusion of non-residents and tourists from TUS sampling frames makes the data blind to guest behavior.7 guest schedules must be modeled using tourism board statistics and localized hospitality registers.7  
2. **Hospitals and Inpatient Healthcare Facilities:** Institutionalized populations are out of the TUS frame, and patient care routines do not follow typical household patterns.35 Occupancy must be derived from hospital bed-occupancy registers, shift rosters, and clinical flow data.36  
3. **High-Density Multi-Tenant Offices with Hybrid Policies:** Post-pandemic remote-work volatility makes historical TUS datasets highly inaccurate.43 Models require real-time local calibrations utilizing building-level badge-access tracking, Wi-Fi positioning systems, or environmental CO2 monitoring.33  
4. **Transit Hubs (Airports, Train Stations):** These spaces are dominated by transient, out-of-region, and tourist populations who do not reside in the target sample.7 Schedules must be constructed utilizing airline flight schedules, passenger throughput registers, and public transit tap-in data.  
5. **Large-Scale Retail Malls and Shopping Centers:** The low resolution of aggregated TUS shopping activities, combined with severe temporal recall rounding, fails to capture the true fluid dynamic of mall traffic.3 This must be augmented or replaced with mobile location data (GPS pings) and physical pedestrian counter logs.3
