# Office Occupancy vs. WFH Trends (2019–2030)

**Measured occupancy (pre- vs. post-COVID):**  Before the pandemic, office attendance was treated as 100% baseline (Feb 2020=100% occupancy).  In practice, pre-2020 daily attendance on peak days was typically 80–100% of assigned seating.  Since COVID, in-person occupancy collapsed.  For example, Kastle Systems (keycard data) reports **peak-day** attendance in major US cities at only ~62% of Feb‑2020 levels (Mar 2024), with **average weekly occupancy ~52–55%** of the same baseline.  XY Sense sensor data similarly show current *global* workspace occupancy ≈40% of capacity (Q4 2024–Q1 2025).  Cushman & Wakefield and CBRE find overall utilization stabilized at ~50–60% of pre‑pandemic levels by 2024.  

**Weekly/diurnal patterns:**  All data show a pronounced midweek peak: Tuesdays (and often Wednesdays) are busiest, while Mondays and especially Fridays are much lighter.  Kastle’s “hybrid peak” (Tue–Thu) average is ~58% of pre‑COVID occupancy (2023), with Tuesdays reaching ~60% and Fridays ~30% (only ~43% below the midweek peak).  An Axios analysis similarly finds occupancy ≈62% on peak days vs. ~35% on Fridays (Mar 2024).  Surveys and sensor indices (e.g. VergeSense) confirm **Tuesday–Thursday** highest usage, with Mondays 30–40% below midweek and Fridays <50% of midweek.  Diurnal data show a two-peaked day: arrivals cluster 9–11 AM (peak ~11 AM) with a midday drop, a second peak ~2 PM, and a rapid decline after ~4 PM.  

**Measured utilization:**  Most offices remain *over-desked*.  Even with ~50% attendance, available workstations are far underused: e.g. XY Sense finds ~25% of desks never used and 19% used <1 hour/day.  In short, typical modern offices see 50–60% of employees in the building on the busiest days, far lower on Mondays/Fridays, and many spaces sit empty. This holds in Canada as well; while direct Canadian occupancy sensor data are scarce, Statistics Canada reports **stable** ~20% of workers WFH by late 2023 (implying ~80% office-attendance potential), comparable to US patterns.  

## 2. Canadian WFH Trends (2019–2024)  

Canadian labour surveys show a huge COVID spike in homeworking.  Only ~7–8% of Canadians “usually” worked mainly from home pre-pandemic (e.g. January–Feb 2020).  In April 2020 this leapt to ~40% (strict lockdown) and then receded as offices re-opened.  By January 2022 about **30%** of workers were still doing most hours at home, and by late 2023 that share was ~20%.  (For example, StatCan reports ~41% WFH in Apr 2020; ≈30% by Jan 2022; ≈20% by Nov 2023.)  In May 2021, about 24% of Canadians “usually” worked at home (up from 7.1% in 2016).  By mid‑2023 it was ~20%.  

By comparison, US levels of regular WFH are somewhat higher: U.S. data show 35–40% of employees working remotely (at least some days) by 2022.  European rates remain lower – Eurostat data in 2021 show only ~12% of EU workers “usually” WFH.  (For example, Finland topped EU at 25%, but many countries are <10%.)  Thus **Canada/US ~20–40% WFH** vs **EU ~10–20%** in recent years.  

An approximate timeline:  
- **2019:** ≈7% in Canada (pre-pandemic baseline).  
- **2020:** Sharp rise – ~40% in April 2020.  
- **2021:** Roughly 25–30%. (StatCan: ~30% Jan 2022, implying similar late-2021.)  
- **2022:** ~23–24% (year average). (For example, Dec 2022 ~22.1%.)  
- **2023:** ~20% (Nov 2023 ~19.7%).  
- **2024:** Estimated ~15–20% (e.g. Jan 2024 ~16% in LFS).  

(*Sources:* Statistics Canada Labour Force Survey data.)  

## 3. Forecasts to 2030 (Office Occupancy & WFH Scenarios)  

**Real-estate/energy-modeling projections:**  Most analyses foresee *partial* return-to-office, not full.  McKinsey (2023) estimates that **office demand** in the “median city” will be **~0–13% lower by 2030** than in 2019, reflecting longer-term hybrid adoption.  (In a survey of 9 global cities, McKinsey projected demand drops of ~16–20% in tech hubs, small increases in a few cities.)  In other words, even with hybrid work, many companies will hold 80–90% of their pre‑COVID space.  Moody’s cites a McKinsey study: “13% less demand for office space in the median city by 2030”. 

Energy or buildings models that incorporate occupancy similarly assume a range of hybrid levels.  For example, building simulations often reduce peak occupant densities by a “remote-work factor” to estimate loads (see next section).  

**2030 WFH scenarios:**  We define three illustrative scenarios for 2030:  

- **“Conservative Return” (Low WFH)** – Office usage recovers almost fully.  Only ~15–20% of workdays are remote by 2030 (i.e. 80–85% in-office attendance).  This could reflect strong RTO mandates or structural shifts back to in-person work.  It corresponds to only a small additional decline from today’s ~20% WFH.  

- **“Hybrid Normal” (Mid WFH)** – Hybrid stabilizes at moderate levels.  Approximately **30%** of workdays are remote (70% in office).  This aligns with trends (Stanford projects ~25→35% WFH by 2035) and McKinsey’s median city outlook (≈13% drop in space). It implies offices at ~75–80% of 2019 attendance by 2030.  

- **“Fully Hybrid” (High WFH)** – Remote becomes dominant for many.  ~40% of workdays are remote (60% in-office).  This assumes continued technology shifts and employee preference.  It lies at the high end of forecasts (e.g. Stanford’s 35% by 2035 suggests ~30–35% by 2030).  

Each scenario implies a corresponding range of occupancy: e.g. if all employees have 5 desk/week, then 20% WFH means 80% desks used on average.  We will test these as a single “WFH rate” lever.  McKinsey’s finding that ~0% demand loss median by 2030 corresponds roughly to our “Hybrid Normal” (30% WFH) scenario, while high-remote forecasts (35–40%) drive occupancy down near the “Fully Hybrid” case.  

**Canada vs. US/EU:**  Canadian projections are broadly similar to the U.S. case.  Canadian employers expect to allow significant WFH (e.g. surveys of large firms often target 3 days/week in office), so our scenarios apply equally.  EU countries may skew lower (EU WFH was ~12% in 2021), so a Canadian/EU note: *Europe’s return-to-office has lagged North America’s, so Canadian occupancy may recover slightly faster and to a higher level than EU norms, all else equal.*  

## 4. Energy Demand Consequences  

Hybrid/work-from-home reduces office loads but **not proportionally**.  A building left half-empty still uses much of its base energy (HVAC, lighting, servers, etc.).  For example, OccuSpace notes that “HVAC and pumps still run like the building’s full [and] lighting often stays on all day” when occupancy dips.  Unless systems are dynamically adjusted, a 50% headcount drop might yield far less than 50% energy savings.  

Conversely, buildings with occupancy sensors or smart controls can turn off lights and reduce HVAC in empty zones.  In one simulation of a “Zero Emission” office building, adding remote days cut annual **energy use intensity (EUI)** up to ~46%.  (That case assumed comprehensive controls and two remote days/week.) In practice, partial reductions in peak load and energy can be achieved, but fixed schedules (e.g. minimum ventilation) and plug loads create a baseline demand.  Thus **energy modeling should reflect these nonlinearities**: e.g. reduce peak loads and occupancy-driven gains, but retain a nonzero overnight/daytime base.  

Load-shape effects:  More work-at-home shifts demand from commercial to residential hours.  The **office peak** (mid-morning, midweek) flattens when many workers stay home, reducing peak office electricity demand (lighting/compute) but often *raising* grid demand elsewhere.  HVAC peaks (cooling/heating) may also shift (colder nights demand heat at home, less at office). Energy models therefore track occupancy schedules carefully.  

In sum, studies find ~20–50% occupancy reductions typically yield 10–30% building energy savings, not one-to-one.  For example, the Norwegian study showed 2 remote days/week gave ~46% EUI drop (almost linear), but OccuSpace warns that without controls the same headcount drop wastes energy on “empty air”.  

## 5. Representing WFH in Energy Models  

We treat “WFH rate” as a single **scenario parameter** that scales office occupancy schedules.  Concretely, one can reduce the baseline occupancy profile (e.g. 100% presence on Tue/Wed) by the WFH fraction.  For example, a 30% WFH rate means only 70% of seats are filled on average.  In models (e.g. EnergyPlus or stock building models), this can be implemented by multiplying occupant densities or by omitting occupants on certain days.  Some studies explicitly construct “occupancy scenarios” (e.g. 10 scenarios from full-time on-site to extensive remote). For our forecasts, we will vary the WFH parameter (0–50%) to test sensitivity.
