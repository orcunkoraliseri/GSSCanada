# **Data-Driven Occupancy Modelling for Building and Urban Energy Simulation: A Systematic Review of Paradigms, Datasets, and Open Challenges**

## **The Evolution of Occupancy Representation in Building Energy Simulations**

Traditional paradigms in building energy simulation (BEM) and urban building energy modelling (UBEM) have historically treated occupants as static, passive entities.1 Standard model archetypes represent human presence using simplified, deterministic schedules derived from national building codes and reference standards, such as ASHRAE Standard 90.1, the National Energy Code of Canada for Buildings (NECB), or the Department of Energy (DOE) prototype commercial building models.2 Under these frameworks, building occupants are quantified merely as constant sensible and latent heat loads, carbon dioxide (![][image1]) emitters, and passive triggers for mechanical ventilation or lighting.1  
However, actual energy use patterns in commercial, institutional, and mixed-use structures exhibit profound stochasticity.3 Modern work dynamics—accelerated by hot-desking, teleworking, and flexible hybrid office schedules—have decoupled occupancy from fixed architectural timers.8 The use of homogeneous schedules across uniform archetypes introduces a profound "performance gap" between simulated and actual building energy consumption.4 For example, applying deterministic schedules uniformly to an entire district assumes simultaneous peak occupant densities across all spaces of a given type.12 This overestimates peak localized thermal loads and neglects the spatial-temporal diversity inherent in human mobility.12  
To close this performance gap, researchers are leveraging advanced data science, IoT infrastructure, and machine learning to construct dynamic, occupant-centric models.1 Occupancy representation is defined across a hierarchy of four granular levels:

1. The total headcount within a building over time (![][image2]),  
2. The binary occupancy status (vacant or occupied) of a specific thermal zone or space (![][image3]),  
3. The precise headcount within a distinct zone or space (![][image4]),  
4. The exact spatial location of an individual occupant (![][image5]).12

Developing precise models at these levels is particularly critical for non-residential and mixed-use buildings (such as offices, retail spaces, and university complexes) where HVAC and lighting systems are the primary energy consumers, yet operate under highly erratic, multi-zone occupancy regimes.9

## **Main Modelling Paradigms and Integration Frameworks**

Modern occupancy research for building and urban energy simulations is categorized into five main modelling paradigms. These paradigms range from standardized, rule-based baselines to highly stochastic, sensor-driven, and generative deep-learning systems.

### **Deterministic Standard Schedules**

Deterministic schedules represent the oldest and most widely implemented paradigm in commercial simulation practices. These schedules rely on fixed, hourly fraction profiles (diversity factors) specified by standards such as ASHRAE 90.1, the National Energy Code of Canada for Buildings (NECB), and the DOE Commercial Prototype Models.2 The modeler scales these daily profiles according to design-day parameters or weekday/weekend schedules.6  
In tools like EnergyPlus and DOE-2, these schedules are defined as direct, continuous decimal fractions $f(t) \\in $ mapped to maximum occupant densities.19 While computationally trivial and highly standardized, this approach fails to capture the stochasticity of occupants, often leading to oversizing of HVAC equipment and inaccurate peak load forecasts.3

### **Stochastic Markov-Chain and Agent-Based Models**

To capture the inherent randomness of human behavior, stochastic paradigms simulate occupancy as a random process. Standard formulations utilize the Page et al. heterogeneous Markov-chain model or Wang's homogeneous Markov-chain model to determine the movement of occupants between discrete states (e.g., present or absent, or transitioning between offices, corridors, and meeting rooms).13 These models transition state variables ![][image6] using transition probability matrices:

In agent-based models (ABMs), each occupant and room is modeled as an independent agent governed by rule-based and probabilistic behaviors (such as Reinhart's LIGHTSWITCH-2002 model for arrival/departure and custom conference room meeting models).13 These stochastic engines generate sub-hourly profiles (typically at 5, 10, or 15-minute resolutions).13  
To integrate these stochastic outputs into whole-building engines, tools like the Occupant Behavior XML (obXML) schema and the Occupant Behavior Functional Mock-up Unit (obFMU) are utilized.22 The obFMU implements the Functional Mock-up Interface (FMI) standard, allowing co-simulation where EnergyPlus passes real-time environmental conditions (e.g., indoor temperature, ![][image1] level) to the obFMU, and the obFMU returns updated occupancy, lighting, and HVAC setpoint schedules at each time step.22

### **Survey and Time-Use-Driven Models**

This paradigm utilizes large-scale, public-domain Time Use Surveys (TUS), such as the UK Time Use Survey or the Belgium TUS, to construct realistic household or workplace activity profiles.6 Qualitative data (e.g., diaries recording activities at 10-minute intervals) are transformed into quantitative schedules through hierarchical clustering and statistical sampling.6  
These models capture the demographic diversity of building occupants (e.g., administrative staff versus research faculty in institutional buildings) and translate their reported activities into thermal heat gains, domestic hot water demands, and appliance usage patterns.6 The schedules are formatted as external CSV files and read directly by EnergyPlus using the Schedule:File input object, allowing district-scale simulations (UBEM) to bypass repetitive code execution while preserving population-wide behavioral variance.25

### **Sensor, Wi-Fi, and ![][image1]\-Driven Data Models**

With the ubiquity of IoT infrastructure in modern commercial buildings, empirical data-driven models are increasingly used.9 These models utilize passive Wi-Fi sniffing (identifying connected and advertising media access control addresses), carbon dioxide (![][image1]) sensors, mmWave radar, low-resolution thermal cameras, and pressure-sensitive flooring to detect or estimate headcount in real time.9  
For instance, ![][image1]\-based machine learning models utilize differential pressures between spaces and historical HVAC ventilation states to estimate occupant numbers, overcoming localized ![][image1] transport lag.31 Alternatively, Wi-Fi probe data are fed into multivariate fuzzy radial basis function neural networks (M-FRNN) to predict zone-level headcount, saving up to 55% of ventilation energy when integrated into demand-controlled ventilation (DCV) loops in EnergyPlus.30 These empirical models bypass theoretical state transitions, converting real-time sensor streams into immediate operational profiles for active building energy management system (BEMS) optimization.9

### **Deep-Learning Generative Approaches**

Recent advances in generative deep learning have introduced models capable of synthesizing highly realistic multivariate time-series profiles of occupancy, power demands, and building operations without relying on explicit physics-based rules.33 Prominent architectures include Variational Autoencoders (VAEs), Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs), and diffusion-based models like Tabular Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models (TabDDPM).10  
For example, the Regularized Adversarial Fine-Tuned VAE-GAN (RAFT-VG) hybridizes VAE and GAN architectures, utilizing an encoder-decoder backbone initialized via reconstruction loss, then adversarially fine-tuned with a discriminator to prevent mode collapse.33 Generative models can synthesize realistic multi-channel time-series (e.g., synchronized zone headcounts, lighting loads, and plug loads) at sub-hourly resolutions.33  
To interface these PyTorch-trained generative networks with physics-based building envelope models (such as those in the Modelica Buildings library), developers are bypassing the FMI standard's co-simulation overhead.37 Instead, they export the deep network's inferenced weights to highly optimized C/C++ libraries, calling the generator directly during Modelica execution via external C function declarations (genCTS).37 This keeps simulation runtime overhead minimal compared to running the thermal model itself.37

## **Mixed-Use Consistency and Multi-Building Frameworks**

Simulating multi-functional or mixed-use commercial towers (e.g., podium structures combining retail, office space, and hotel guest rooms) under a single shared framework introduces significant model integration and behavioral alignment challenges. The primary obstacle is maintaining logical, physical, and spatial consistency across these diverse zones without generating conflicting operational profiles.4  
Researchers utilize two distinct conceptual frameworks to handle these challenges:

* **Shared Backbone Frameworks**: The shared backbone approach relies on a centralized, higher-level data generator that models human mobility, and then projects these coordinated trajectories down into individual building uses.4 For example, in a massive urban simulation across Phoenix, Arizona encompassing over 700 buildings (including offices, retail communities, schools, and churches), researchers collected GPS trajectory data from 2,461 unique users.10 Rather than training isolated models for each building type, the researchers established a shared backbone by performing time-series K-means clustering on the temporal features of all users.10 This identified nine distinct human mobility clusters.10 Dedicated LSTM-Wasserstein GANs (WGANs) were then trained on these clusters.10 By mapping the synthesized trajectories to physical locations using GIS buffers (e.g., a 50-meter spatial join around verified 3D building footprints), the framework generated synchronized arrival, duration, and departure schedules.10 Because the profiles originate from a unified mobility distribution, the arrival of office workers matches the departure from residential spaces, maintaining systemic consistency across the simulated district.10 Similarly, the tabular diffusion framework (TabDDPM) trained on ResStock models millions of buildings by capturing complex, mixed-type multivariate conditional distributions over a single conditional backbone.36 This backbone simultaneously synthesizes spatial geometry, local appliance specifications, and matching occupant counts, preventing unrealistic edge cases (e.g., high occupant counts assigned to extremely small floor areas).36  
* **Separate Models per Use (Component-Based Integration)**: In contrast, component-based architectures utilize distinct, dedicated models optimized for individual building uses (e.g., a Markov model for offices, a queueing model for retail, and a time-use sampler for residential zones) and merge their schedules within the physics-based simulation engine.7 The major challenge in this approach is maintaining inter-zone physical constraints. To resolve this, the obXML and obFMU frameworks implement the standardized DNAS (drivers, needs, actions, systems) ontology.22 Within a mixed-use building, separate obFMU instances are instantiated for each zone (e.g., one for the retail floor and one for the office floor).24 Consistency is enforced through a shared co-simulation manager.24 The manager coordinates real-time data exchanges, ensuring that shared physical dependencies—such as centralized HVAC loop constraints, common space ventilation, or multi-zone air infiltration—dynamically react to the independent occupants' actions without violating thermodynamic laws.23

A prominent application of separate stochastic models integrated into archetypes is demonstrated in the Concordia University Sir George Williams (SGW) campus case study in Montreal.17 Researchers integrated standardized 3D geometry models derived from Level of Detail (LoD) 2 CityGML footprints with Concordia's Facilities Management Information System (FMIS) to subdivide buildings into discrete thermal zones.17 To replace standard ASHRAE 90.1 schedules, they applied a unified archetype framework using two different sources of stochastic profiles:

* Smart-metered historical electricity profiles from the university's institutional buildings, decomposed using K-means clustering and reconstructed with Gaussian Processes to capture load uncertainty 7,  
* Stochastic schedules derived from the Building Data Genome Project 2 (BDG2) dataset to assess cross-regional transferability.17

By running simulations within the Tools4Cities platform, they verified that replacing deterministic profiles with stochastic campus-derived schedules yielded a 32.26% improvement in predicting daily load profiles, establishing that local, data-driven archetypes can represent complex, multi-building institutional contexts.17

## **Benchmark Datasets and Open Collaboration Initiatives**

The rapid evolution of data-driven occupancy modelling is supported by several benchmark datasets and international research collaborations. These public resources provide the necessary ground-truth data to train, calibrate, and validate advanced generative and stochastic models.

### **The ASHRAE Global Occupant Behavior Database**

Initiated under IEA EBC Annex 79, this is the most comprehensive repository of field-measured occupant behavior data.42 This database integrates 34 distinct field-measured datasets contributed by 39 institutions across 15 countries and 10 climate zones, covering both commercial and residential sectors.42 The dataset includes:

* Continuous, high-frequency occupant presence logs and headcount data 43,  
* Multi-aspect environmental measurements (indoor/outdoor dry-bulb temperature, relative humidity, illuminance, and ![][image1] concentrations) 42,  
* Logged physical interactions with building interfaces, including light switches, window opening/closing, window blinds, thermostat adjustments, and fan operations.42

To ensure machine-readability and semantic consistency, the database leverages the Brick schema to model sensor networks and spatial metadata.42 This makes it a standard training set for validating machine learning algorithms.42

### **The Building Data Genome Project (BDG)**

The Building Data Genome Project 2 (BDG2) contains annual, hourly sensor readings from over 3,000 non-residential meters across multiple climate zones.17 While primarily focused on whole-building electricity, heating, and cooling demand, its highly resolved load profiles are widely used as proxies for indirect occupancy classification.5 Machine learning workflows use these load profiles to reconstruct occupancy patterns based on the correlation between human presence and lighting/appliance electricity usage.5

### **Time-Use Survey (TUS) Datasets**

Publicly available national time-use surveys, such as the UK Time Use Survey (TUS 2014-15) and the Belgian Time Use Survey, provide high-quality records of human activity.6 These surveys contain thousands of 24-hour diaries logging activities, social contexts, and locations at 10-minute intervals.6 They serve as the statistical foundation for synthesizing occupant density distributions and generating representative residential and workplace archetypes.25

### **IEA EBC Annex 79 and DOE CMT Initiatives**

The International Energy Agency's Energy in Buildings and Communities (IEA EBC) Programme completed **Annex 79 (Occupant-Centric Building Design and Operation, 2018–2024)**.8 Building on Annex 66, Annex 79 focused on integrating occupant-centric models into building controls and design standards, yielding open-source occupant-centric books and a shared model evaluation platform.8  
Concurrently, the US Department of Energy's **Benchmarking Datasets of Building Environmental Conditions and Occupancy Parameters** project—led by the National Laboratory of the Rockies in partnership with PNNL, ORNL, and LBNL—actively curates well-calibrated, multi-sensor operational datasets.48 These datasets compile continuous spatial measurements of equipment health, thermal variables, and headcount.48 They establish empirical baselines to determine the spatial and temporal resolution required to optimize demand-controlled ventilation and load forecasting.48

## **Open Problems and Structural Transitions**

Despite the technical advancements in the field, several fundamental barriers prevent the widespread, scalable implementation of data-driven occupancy models in professional building and urban energy simulation workflows.

### **Transferability Across Building Typologies and Climates**

Occupancy and behavior models are highly localized and culturally dependent.6 A model trained on a specific modern office floor in a temperate climate frequently fails when transferred to different building layouts, occupancy types, or environmental conditions.4 While physical features like u-values or HVAC design can be mapped, human-building interaction profiles (such as comfort setpoints and mobility speeds) do not transfer easily across different regions.4 When researchers attempt to adapt models cross-regionally—such as applying BGDP2-derived archetypes to Concordia's Montreal campus—they observe significant variations in energy predictions.17 This demonstrates that without local fine-tuning, models struggle to capture contextual differences.17

### **The Validation and Ground-Truth Gap**

There is a fundamental challenge in validating stochastic and generative occupancy models.4 Because occupancy is inherently random, direct deterministic comparisons (e.g., comparing predicted daily profiles to single-day measurements) are highly misleading.10 Validating these models requires evaluating statistical distributions over long periods, comparing metrics like Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence, Fréchet Inception Distance (FID), or Coefficient of Variation of the Root Mean Square Error (CVRMSE).7 However, obtaining the long-term, high-resolution ground-truth data required to calculate these metrics is extremely difficult due to the high costs of sensor networks and strict privacy policies.4

### **Data Scarcity in Non-Residential and Commercial Sectors**

While residential occupancy patterns can be modeled using national time-use surveys, non-residential buildings (such as corporate offices, multi-tenant commercial structures, and hotels) face a severe data scarcity problem.7 Private organizations are hesitant to deploy high-resolution camera tracking, Wi-Fi sniffing, or personal location metadata due to strict privacy regulations and liability concerns.9 As a result, most public occupant datasets are limited to single-zone offices or university classrooms, leaving large commercial complexes poorly represented.16 While passive Wi-Fi sensing and mmWave radar offer privacy-preserving alternatives, their deployment in large-scale commercial buildings is still limited.9

### **Structural Breaks, Climate Shifts, and the Hybrid Work Era**

The COVID-19 pandemic and the subsequent transition to hybrid work models have introduced a profound structural break in building operations data.8 Generative and stochastic models trained on pre-pandemic data (pre-2020) suffer from severe data drift, as they assume full, continuous five-day office occupancy.8 This hybrid occupancy structure results in highly irregular weekly load shapes that render classic autoregressive forecasting models obsolete.53 Furthermore, as global temperatures rise and extreme weather events become more frequent, standard occupancy schedules cannot adapt dynamically.10 Under extreme weather conditions, occupant behaviors—such as opening windows, adjusting thermostats, or modifying building-level spatial distribution—undergo sudden changes.1 Static models are incapable of representing these sudden shifts, leading to significant errors during peak heating and cooling periods.12

## **Implications for a Shared Multi-Channel Generative Model**

The findings of this literature review suggest five major implications for the development of a shared, multi-channel generative model (e.g., a diffusion or VAE-GAN framework) designed to generate synchronized occupancy, lighting, equipment, and comfort profiles for BEM/UBEM:

* **Heterogeneous Cross-Channel Integration**: To prevent physical inconsistencies, the generative model must use a unified latent representation to jointly synthesize numerical values (headcounts) and categorical states (HVAC mode, window status, and lighting states) across all simulated zones.34 This ensures that lighting and equipment loads scale logically with simulated occupant headcounts.10  
* **Preserving Dynamic Covariance**: Rather than generating independent schedules, the network must capture the dynamic covariance between internal loads and environmental states.18 When the model generates a high-occupancy event, it must simultaneously output realistic increases in carbon dioxide (![][image1]) concentration, latent heat gains, and appliance power demands.31  
* **Hierarchical Spatial and Temporal Scaling**: The model's latent architecture must be designed to scale seamlessly.4 It should generate long-term, hourly patterns for regional district heating analyses (UBEM) while retaining the ability to sample high-frequency, sub-hourly (10-minute) occupancy fluctuations for individual thermal zones.10  
* **Frictionless, Direct-Execution Integration**: To make generative models practical for engineers, the model's inference engine must bypass FMI co-simulation overhead.37 The architecture should support direct compilation into lightweight, portable binary libraries.37 This allows BEM engines like Modelica or EnergyPlus to call the neural network directly as an internal function during execution, minimizing simulation runtime overhead.37  
* **Built-in Adaptability to Structural Breaks**: The generative architecture must incorporate conditional inputs—such as geographical metadata, workplace policy variables, and real-time indoor-outdoor climate conditions.10 This allows the model to adjust to structural breaks like hybrid work shifts or extreme weather events, ensuring predictions remain accurate under changing climate and operational regimes.4
