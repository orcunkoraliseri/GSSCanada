### Journal Validation Standard and Comparative Assessment
Applied Energy maintains two distinct validation tiers depending on the primary claim of the submission:
- For *macro stock-scale policy and national energy accounting models* (e.g. Chen et al. 2022, Wu et al. 2024), the journal accepts calibration against national survey totals and held-out distributions when interval telemetry is inaccessible at national scale.
- For *papers whose central claimed contribution is hourly or sub-hourly load shape, peak demand timing, and ramping dynamics* (e.g. Claeys et al. 2023, 2024; Ku et al. 2022), the journal expects empirical comparison against measured interval data (such as smart-meter cohorts or utility feeder load telemetry).

**Assessment of our paper:** **THIN.**
Our manuscript is titled *From "How Much" to "When": Forecasting the Residential Energy Load Shape...* and asserts diurnal load-shape reshaping, midday fill, and peak-hour stability as its headline contributions. Yet our empirical validation rests entirely on:
1. Jensen-Shannon divergence and RMSE against held-out time-use diaries (validating occupancy distributions, not energy loads), and
2. Annual equipment and lighting energy matching NRCan SHEU 2019 survey benchmarks within +-2.7% across 48 cells (validating annual kWh magnitude, which is phase-invariant).

We provide zero comparison against measured Canadian hourly electricity load shapes (e.g. utility class profiles from IESO, Hydro-Quebec, or municipal utilities). An Applied Energy reviewer evaluating a paper that claims to solve "When" will immediately identify that annual energy calibration cannot validate diurnal timing. By the journal's standards for load-shape claims, the validation is thin and highly vulnerable to rejection.

## 5. Competing Explanations for Headline Results

### Headline 1: Weekday at-home occupancy breaks +5.2 pp at COVID and persists to 2030 (+2.2 to +3.9 pp)
A substantial portion of this observed shift is plausibly an artifact of survey collection-mode transition rather than pure behavioral evolution, compounded by an uncorrected calibration error admitted by the authors. Between 2010 and 2022, Statistics Canada transitioned the General Social Survey from 100% Computer-Assisted Telephone Interviewing (CATI) to a self-administered electronic questionnaire (EQ). Methodological survey literature establishes that self-administered digital time-use diaries capture significantly higher rates of in-home secondary activities, passive media consumption, and home presence compared to interviewer-administered telephone recall, where respondents disproportionately omit brief at-home episodes. More critically, the authors concede in Section 7 that the 2030 forecast calibration was raked against a pre-relink 2022 frame that carried a higher at-home level, inflating the reported 2022-to-2030 gap (+2.2 to +3.9 pp). Furthermore, post-2022 empirical mobility data (Guo et al., 2026) document an active return-to-office rebound that stabilized in-office days at 29.1% by 2023; assuming 100% WFH persistence through 2030 ignores documented employer return-to-office mandates.

### Headline 2: Annual electricity follows by only +1.4 to +2.6% across the break and +0.6 to +1.2% to 2030
The authors interpret this minimal annual change as a physical finding: "thermal decoupling of internal gains from total electricity in well-insulated Canadian housing stock." In reality, this modest increment is an inevitable artifact of rigid model constraints and fuel assumptions. First, space heating in the Canadian code archetypes is predominantly natural gas-fired; electrical heating was not modeled, so the primary winter thermal load did not register on the electric meter. Second, the authors forced simulated equipment and lighting loads to match fixed annual NRCan SHEU 2019 kWh benchmarks (e.g. 3,700 kWh for SingleDetached) using static scalar multipliers. Third, continuous baseload appliances (refrigerator ~448 kWh, freezer ~343 kWh, standby ~415 kWh; exceeding 1,200 kWh/yr) were held completely invariant 24/7. When 70% to 80% of household electrical consumption is mathematically pinned by unvarying baseloads and annual survey targets, annual electricity is precluded from moving by construction.

### Headline 3: Midday fill and flattening (delta midday share +0.37 pp; delta load factor +0.012) with evening peak fixed at ~17:30
This load-shape behavior can be fully reproduced by a simple, non-behavioral baseline without requiring a 29-million parameter Transformer. Any trivial schedule that injects a small, constant fractional equipment or plug load during daytime hours mathematically produces a midday fill and increases the load factor (mean load divided by peak load). The stability of the evening peak at ~17:30 is not a subtle emergent property of complex occupancy dynamics; it is the physical consequence of cooking, dinner preparation, dishwashing, and domestic lighting routines. Domestic energy literature (Widen and Wackelgard, 2010; Richardson et al., 2010) demonstrates that cooking appliances (stoves, ovens, kettles, microwaves) have rated power draws of 1,500 W to 3,000 W, completely dominating the 70 W to 150 W draws of telework laptops and monitors. Whether an occupant spent the afternoon working in a home office or commuting from a commercial workplace, dinner and evening lighting occur at 17:30 to 19:00. The evening peak stays put because residential kitchen and lighting wattages swamp telework equipment wattages.

### Headline 4: Activity resolution restructures intraday equipment profile without displacing the peak (shift 0 +- 1 h)
The authors present the 0 +- 1 h peak shift as a nuanced finding demonstrating that activity modeling refines profile shape without altering peak timing. However, this null result is a direct mechanical consequence of the 9-end-use by 14-activity crosswalk matrix and the fixed 24/7 baseload. The crosswalk statically couples high-power kitchen equipment and lighting to evening meal preparation and post-dusk wakefulness. Because high-draw activities are hard-coded into evening hours, and daytime telework activities are assigned modest plug loads, the simulated peak hour is mathematically anchored to the evening window by the crosswalk definitions. The activity layer produces zero peak shift because the input assumptions guarantee that no daytime activity possesses sufficient concentrated power to rival evening cooking. A simple presence-filtered schedule produces the exact same peak timing because the underlying load hierarchy is identical.

## 6. Ranked Reviewer Critique (Top 10 Vulnerabilities)

### 1. Admitted Calibration Provenance Defect in Headline 2030 Forecast (Section 7, p. 25, lines 461-462; Abstract, p. 1, line 10; Figure 5)
- **Severity:** would-reject
- **Critique:** In Section 7, the authors openly disclose that the 2030 forecast occupancy calibration was raked against a pre-relink 2022 population, was never updated after the 2026-07-09 household-frame relink, and that the resulting 2022-to-2030 at-home increment (+2.2 to +3.9 pp) and Figure 5 are "inflated" and "provisional." Submitting a manuscript to Applied Energy where the primary forward forecast number featured in the Abstract, Highlights, and Section 5.1 is known and admitted by the authors to be corrupted by an outdated calibration pipeline is fatal. A reviewer will not accept "provisional" numbers in a finalized submission.

### 2. Complete Absence of Empirical Hourly Load-Shape Validation (Section 3.6, Section 4.4, Section 5.3, Figure 6)
- **Severity:** would-reject
- **Critique:** The manuscript's central premise and title promise a methodology for determining "When" energy is used by forecasting diurnal load shapes, load factors, and coincident peak hours. Yet the simulation results are never compared against measured hourly residential electricity consumption data. Calibrating annual equipment totals to SHEU 2019 benchmarks validates only annual magnitude (kWh); annual sums are phase-invariant and provide zero validation of intraday timing, midday fill, or peak hour. Recent Applied Energy papers on residential load profiles (Claeys et al. 2023, 2024; Ku et al. 2022) validate against empirical smart-meter interval datasets. Without an empirical check against measured hourly load shapes (e.g. utility feeder or smart-meter telemetry), the load-shape conclusions are unvalidated simulations.

### 3. Unbounded Single-Scenario 2030 Projection with Complete WFH Persistence (Section 3.4, p. 11; Section 7, p. 26; Section 8, p. 27)
- **Severity:** would-request-major-revision
- **Critique:** The entire 2030 forecast rests on an unconditional deterministic assumption: that peak pandemic work-from-home practices persist with probability one. Recent literature and labor statistics (Guo et al., 2026; Barrero et al., 2021) demonstrate significant post-pandemic return-to-office dynamics and hybrid stabilization. Presenting a single "high-persistence" scenario as "the 2030 forecast" without bounding counter-scenarios (such as partial return-to-office or pre-pandemic reversion) renders the long-term projections speculative.

### 4. Severe, Systematic Under-Prediction of EUI Across All Archetypes (Section 5.2, Table 5, p. 20)
- **Severity:** would-request-major-revision
- **Critique:** In Table 5, simulated annual Energy Use Intensity falls substantially below the lower bound of NRCan SHEU regional-average benchmark ranges for all four dwelling archetypes (SingleDetached is 12% below, OtherDwelling 27% below, MidRise 3% below, HighRise 31% below). The authors dismiss this failure by arguing that their archetypes model new-code (NECB 2017 / NBC 9.36) envelopes whereas SHEU reflects older existing stock. However, the paper claims to model stock-scale residential energy demand. If the archetypes represent only post-2017 construction, the stock model cannot represent the standing Canadian building stock, invalidating its claims to national stock representation.

### 5. Failure to Benchmark Against Simpler Baseline Schedules (Section 1.1, Section 3.2, Section 5.4)
- **Severity:** would-request-major-revision
- **Critique:** The paper constructs a massive, computationally expensive generative modeling architecture (a 29.25-million parameter conditional Transformer screened across 40+ trials) and an activity crosswalk, but fails to benchmark it against simpler, established baselines, such as an empirical mean occupancy schedule, a standard diversity profile, or a basic Markov chain. Without demonstrating that the complex deep generative model provides measurable improvements in load factor, peak timing, or ramping accuracy over a standard average schedule, the methodological complexity is unjustified.

### 6. Disjoint Household Panels Breaking the Longitudinal Attribution Design (Section 4.3, Table 4, p. 16; Section 7, p. 25; Figure S8)
- **Severity:** would-request-major-revision
- **Critique:** The study's central methodological argument is that a paired frozen-frame Monte Carlo design isolates pure behavioral effects by holding dwelling physics, climate, and household identities constant. However, due to a mid-project frame relink (2026-07-09), the 2005-2015 panel and the 2022-2030 panel use completely different sets of sampled households. Consequently, the longitudinal comparison across the COVID structural break (2015 to 2022) is not a paired within-household comparison, but a cross-sectional comparison across independent samples, forfeiting the claimed attribution advantage for the most important historical transition.

### 7. Spatial Over-Generalization of a Single Montreal Zone 6 Envelope (Section 4.1, Table 3, p. 15; Section 7, p. 25)
- **Severity:** would-request-major-revision
- **Critique:** The simulation applies a single Montreal (Zone 6A) building envelope geometry and construction across all six climate zones, ranging from mild Pacific Zone 5C (Vancouver) to severe subarctic Zone 7A (Winnipeg). In Winnipeg, where heating degree days are extreme, thermal loads, envelope heat loss, and internal gain utilization differ fundamentally from Montreal. Applying an identical Zone 6 envelope to Winnipeg and Vancouver distorts the thermodynamic interaction between occupant heat gains and HVAC operations, undermining cross-climate comparisons.

### 8. Survey Mode Transition Confounded with Behavioral Non-Stationarity (Section 2.1, Table 2, p. 8; Section 7, p. 26)
- **Severity:** would-request-major-revision
- **Critique:** Between the 2010 and 2022 GSS cycles, Statistics Canada shifted its collection methodology from telephone interviewing (CATI) to an online self-administered electronic questionnaire (EQ). Time-use survey methodology literature confirms that self-administered online diaries report higher at-home and passive time than telephone recall. The authors attribute the entire demographically standardized +5.2 pp shift to COVID and work-from-home, without providing empirical evidence to isolate the survey mode effect from genuine behavioral change.

### 9. Factually Inaccurate Capability Matrix Claims (Section 1.2, Table 1, p. 4; Ref Motuziene et al. 2022)
- **Severity:** minor
- **Critique:** Table 1 scores Motuziene et al. (2022) as satisfying "Forecast to future year = Yes" and cites it in Section 1.3 as evidence that occupancy prediction models degrade across the break. However, Motuziene et al. (2022) is an empirical study of commercial office buildings analyzing short-term sensor-based HVAC control during pandemic restrictions; it never forecasts to a future year (such as 2030) and does not model residential buildings. Misrepresenting competitor papers in a capability matrix undermines scholarly rigor.

### 10. Inclusion of Internal Debugging Narratives and QA Artifacts (Section 4.2, p. 15; Section 4.4, p. 17; Section 7, p. 24)
- **Severity:** minor
- **Critique:** The manuscript devotes multiple paragraphs across Methods, Results, and Limitations to narrating an internal 4-hour clock-shift implementation error (04:00 to 00:00 phase misalignment) that was discovered and fixed during simulation runs. Detailed accounts of retired software bugs and laboratory re-runs belong in code commit messages or internal technical reports, not in the main text of an archival Applied Energy paper. It clutters the narrative and raises concerns regarding overall code quality control.
