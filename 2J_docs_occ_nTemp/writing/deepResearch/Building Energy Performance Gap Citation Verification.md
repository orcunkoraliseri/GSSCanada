# **Comprehensive Citation Verification and Temporal Analysis of Residential Energy Performance Gaps**

## **Deterministic Baseline Errors and Grid-Interactive Integration**

The building energy performance gap is a documented phenomenon wherein design-stage calculated predictions diverge significantly from post-occupancy operational measurements.1 While historical building physics research focused primarily on material deterioration, thermal bridging, and HVAC design inefficiencies to explain this discrepancy, contemporary literature identifies human-building interactions as a dominant driver of predictive uncertainty.4 Traditional building energy modeling protocols rely on deterministic, static schedules of occupancy, lighting, and plug loads, which are typically derived from standardized databases such as ASHRAE 90.1 or ISO guidelines.7 These baselines conceptualize occupants as passive thermal loads, completely masking the dynamic, adaptive nature of human behavior, such as interacting with localized thermostat setpoints, operable windows, shading systems, and high-load household appliances.9  
As building envelopes undergo optimization through high-performance insulation and advanced glazing, the relative share of energy uncertainty dictated by occupant behavior increases dramatically.5 With physical envelope losses minimized, internal heat gains and user-driven ventilation actions become the primary determinants of transient thermal behavior.5 This shifts the focus of building performance simulation from annual energy volume estimations, measured in ![][image1], to time-differentiated instantaneous power demand, measured in ![][image2].14 Static, rule-based schedules introduce a flat representation of occupancy that fails to capture the stochastic diversity of human schedules.4 While such schedules may occasionally align with seasonal heating or cooling volumes on an annual basis, they systematically distort the intraday timing, peak coincidences, and load shape profiles of residential demand.16  
This temporal misalignment has severe implications for grid-interactive efficient buildings and localized decarbonization strategies, particularly in winter-peaking, high-latitude regions such as Canada.18 Residential electricity demand in cold climates exhibits highly sensitive, weather-dependent peak windows, typically concentrated between the morning hours of 6:00 AM and 9:00 AM, and evening hours of 4:00 PM and 8:00 PM, driven by domestic routines and heating demands.19 Applying deterministic, static schedules to simulate these systems masks the highly variable and diverse load shapes observed in real-world households.16 Consequently, simulations overestimate or underestimate peak localized grid stress, directly causing the oversizing of heating equipment, the miscalculation of demand response program capacities, and suboptimal thermal storage operations.16 Replacing static schedules with stochastic, empirical models of human-building interaction is therefore a technical prerequisite for reliable localized grid balancing, peak-shaving, and energy policy evaluations.18

## **Target-by-Target Verification Blocks**

### **Target 1: de Wilde (2014)**

* CITATION: de Wilde, P. (2014). The gap between predicted and measured energy performance of buildings: A framework for investigation. Automation in Construction, 41, 40–49.  
* DOI/URL: [https://doi.org/10.1016/j.autcon.2014.02.009](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.autcon.2014.02.009)  
* SUPPORTS (our claim): The foundational definition and general framework of the building energy performance gap between design-stage computed energy predictions and operational measured energy use, noting that actual operational energy use can exceed predictions by up to ![][image3] times.3  
* DIRECT QUOTE: "There often is a significant difference between predicted (computed) energy performance of buildings and actual measured energy use once buildings are operational." (location: Abstract / Page 40\)  
* CONFIDENCE: high  
* CONTRADICTS / CAVEAT: Systematic reviews and Expert evaluations highlight that the performance gap is highly dynamic, multivariate, and cannot be attributed solely to occupant behavior.12 Physical construction errors, poor insulation, air leakage, uncalibrated mechanical control settings, and inadequate post-occupancy system optimization also contribute significantly to the observed discrepancies.2

* CITATION: Yan, D., O'Brien, W., Hong, T., Feng, X., Gunay, H. H. B., Tahmasebi, F., & Mahdavi, A. (2015). Occupant behavior modeling for building performance simulation: Current state and future challenges. Energy and Buildings, 107, UNVERIFIED — pages not in snippets.

* SUPPORTS (our claim): Position of occupant behavior as a widely acknowledged major contributing factor and primary source of uncertainty in building performance simulations.  
* DIRECT QUOTE: "Occupant behavior is now widely recognized as a major contributing factor to uncertainty of building performance." (location: Abstract)

* CONTRADICTS / CAVEAT: The publishing venue is *Energy and Buildings*, volume 107, which corrects the draft's assumption that it was *Building and Environment*.6 Furthermore, literature reviews warn against using occupant behavior as a scapegoat, noting that there is no statistically sufficient evidence proving that occupants are responsible for the "bulk" of building-related energy performance gaps compared to engineering or technical system failures.21
