# **Stochastic Modeling of Residential Energy Demand: Integrating Time-Use Activities, Appliance Characteristics, and National Statistics**

## **Introduction**

The accurate simulation of residential building performance requires highly resolved, dynamic representations of internal thermal and electrical loads. Historically, building energy simulation engines such as EnergyPlus have relied upon static, deterministic fractional schedules to represent lighting, plug loads, and appliance usage. These standard schedules, often derived from generalized macro-level engineering assumptions, assume homogeneous behavioral patterns across all dwellings and occupant demographics. However, deterministic schedules fundamentally fail to capture the stochastic reality of occupant behavior, resulting in an inability to accurately predict peak load coincidence, the potential for demand-side load shifting, and the realistic diurnal fluctuations necessary for advanced grid-interactive efficient building (GEB) analysis.  
To resolve these limitations, modern bottom-up demand modeling relies on Activity-Based Stochastic Load Models. These models utilize Time-Use Survey (TUS) data—comprehensive sociological datasets recording the minute-by-minute activities of individuals—to predict occupant states. These discrete states are subsequently mapped to energy-consuming activities, appliance switch-on events, and continuous power profiles. For the purpose of constructing a robust, activity-driven residential EnergyPlus model based on a 30-minute time-series per household, the integration of established stochastic frameworks, rigorous end-use taxonomies, baseline thermodynamic loads, and macro-level statistical anchoring is absolutely essential.  
The translation of a sociological dataset into a physical thermodynamic schedule necessitates a clear methodological crosswalk. Occupant activities do not translate directly into watts; rather, they serve as behavioral triggers that modulate the probability of specific appliances drawing power. Furthermore, a substantial portion of residential electricity demand is entirely decoupled from human activity, driven instead by always-on phantom loads and thermostatically controlled refrigeration cycles. Finally, the stochastic aggregation of these individual events must be mathematically constrained to align with empirical reality.  
This comprehensive report provides an exhaustive synthesis of the methodologies required to translate a 14-category occupant activity taxonomy into precise EnergyPlus schedules. Furthermore, it details the mechanisms for handling activity-independent baseloads, scaling for household co-presence utilizing economic equivalence theories, calibrating the resultant stochastic profiles to the Natural Resources Canada (NRCan) Survey of Household Energy Use (SHEU) 2019 dataset, and implementing critical validation frameworks such as Dynamic Time Warping.

## **1\. Established Methodologies in Activity-Based Stochastic Load Modeling**

The foundational premise of stochastic bottom-up energy modeling is that residential electricity consumption is a direct, albeit probabilistic, consequence of human activities performed within the dwelling. Rather than assigning a rigid schedule to a television or an oven, these models simulate the human beings inside the dwelling, allowing the appliances to react to the simulated human states. Two of the most preeminent frameworks in this domain are the CREST Demand Model and the Markov-chain approach by Widén and Wäckelgård. Both of these models provide the mathematical scaffolding necessary to link a 30-minute time-series of activity to an EnergyPlus plug load schedule.

### **1.1 The CREST Demand Model**

The Centre for Renewable Energy Systems Technology (CREST) model, developed at Loughborough University, represents a paradigm shift in high-resolution residential load generation. The seminal methodology was published by authors Ian Richardson, Murray Thomson, David Infield, and Clifford in the 2010 paper titled *Domestic electricity use: A high-resolution energy demand model*, published in the journal *Energy and Buildings* (Volume 42, Issue 10, Pages 1878-1887, DOI: 10.1016/j.enbuild.2010.05.023).1 Operating at a highly granular 1-minute temporal resolution, the CREST model generates synthetic electricity demand data by simulating the use of major domestic appliances based on empirical active occupancy patterns.1  
The architecture of the CREST model is defined by a bottom-up, activity-based structure that utilizes stochastic programming techniques to represent dwelling diversity and variance.4 The methodology proceeds through several distinct operational layers, moving from sociology to electrical engineering:  
The first mechanism is the generation of an active occupancy profile. The model establishes when residents are both at home and awake.1 This relies on previously established probability distributions of human presence derived from the UK Time Use Survey. By filtering out periods of sleep or absence, the model isolates the specific temporal windows where human-appliance interaction is physically possible.  
Following the establishment of active occupancy, the model executes the critical appliance-activity mapping phase. Appliances whose operation is dependent upon a particular activity taking place are assigned to relevant activity profiles within the model's registry.8 However, the occurrence of an activity does not mandate the operation of an appliance. For example, if the activity is "cooking," the model maps multiple appliances to this state—such as an electric hob, an oven, a microwave, and a kettle.8 The activity state simply serves as a gating mechanism; when the cooking activity is active, it elevates the probability that one or more of these appliances will experience a switch-on event, but it does not guarantee simultaneous operation.  
To determine the precise moment an appliance activates, the model employs a random number generation process compared against an appliance-specific probability threshold.10 This threshold is defined by a "calibration scalar".10 Because the model uses stochastic selection, the calibration scalar must be mathematically adjusted so that, over a statistically significant number of Monte Carlo simulation runs (or across an annualized 8760-hour profile), the mean annual consumption of the appliance aligns precisely with macroscopic empirical data.8  
Once a switch-on event is triggered by the scalar and the random number generator, the model executes a predefined power level and duration profile for that specific appliance.12 Rather than assuming a constant average power draw, the CREST model injects highly resolved signatures. If a washing machine is triggered, the model replicates the precise temporal sequence of the machine's operation, incorporating the massive initial power spike for water heating, followed by the lower-wattage drum rotation, and concluding with the high-speed motor spinning cycle. By utilizing these physical appliance profiles, the CREST model isolates behavioral plug loads, preventing massive background thermal loads from obscuring the highly volatile behavioral variance that characterizes residential electricity demand.13

### **1.2 The Stochastic Markov-Chain Model**

While the CREST model relies heavily on predefined active occupancy profiles, the framework introduced by Joakim Widén and Ewa Wäckelgård directly simulates the transitional behavior of the occupants. Their methodology was detailed in the 2010 paper titled *A high-resolution stochastic model of domestic activity patterns and electricity demand*, published in the journal *Applied Energy* (Volume 87, Issue 6, Pages 1880-1892, DOI: 10.1016/j.apenergy.2009.11.006).14 This methodology utilizes non-homogeneous Markov chains tuned to extensive empirical time-use data to generate both synthetic activity sequences of individual household members and the resultant electricity demand.14  
The operational mechanics of the Widén and Wäckelgård model are defined by discrete state transitions. The model assumes that an individual can exist in one of several discrete states (e.g., inactive, away, engaging in specific household tasks, sleeping) at any given time step.15 The progression from time ![][image1] to time ![][image2] is governed by a transition probability matrix derived directly from national time-use surveys.15 Because human behavior is heavily time-dependent—for instance, the transition from "sleeping" to "personal care" is highly probable at 07:00 but highly improbable at 03:00—the Markov chains are non-homogeneous. This means the transition matrices are constantly evolving throughout the 24-hour cycle, accurately capturing the diurnal rhythms of residential life.17  
Upon transitioning into a new activity state, the model must determine how long the occupant remains engaged in that behavior. To achieve this, the model samples a Probability Density Function (PDF) corresponding to that specific state to determine the duration of the activity.18 This ensures a realistic temporal spread of activities, successfully capturing both short-duration events like making a coffee and long-duration events like watching a film or engaging in prolonged home office work.14  
The final step is the translation of these simulated behavioral sequences into energy demand. Widén and Wäckelgård establish distinct end-use configurations for different dwelling types, differentiating the baseline infrastructure of detached houses from apartments.16 They assign specific power ratings to the activities, mapping the sociological states to physical loads. The resultant model yields high-fidelity profiles that have been extensively validated against metered data. The model successfully replicates end-use composition, load coincidence, short time-scale fluctuations, and the extreme diversity observed between different households.14  
Both the CREST model and the Widén and Wäckelgård model establish a fundamental rule for activity-based modeling: mapping an activity to a switch-on event requires an intermediary probabilistic step. An activity denotes a *vulnerability* or an *opportunity* for an appliance to operate, rather than a deterministic guarantee. The power level is subsequently assigned based on the typical operational cycle of the specific appliance class triggered by that activity.

The provided 14-category taxonomy must be systematically mapped to distinct, trackable appliance groups: cooking (range/oven/microwave), dishwasher, clothes washer/dryer, TV/entertainment, computer/home-office, personal-care/hot-water, lighting, and states that imply "no active load."  
In the context of the EnergyPlus simulation engine, these appliance activations serve to modulate schedule objects (such as Schedule:File, Schedule:Compact, or Schedule:Year). These schedules are linked directly to ElectricEquipment and Lights objects defined within the building geometry. When the schedule fraction increases above zero, EnergyPlus injects internal heat gains into the thermal zones, altering the sensible and latent cooling loads that the HVAC system must resolve. Therefore, assigning the correct power levels and understanding the convective versus radiant fractions of these loads is as critical as determining the switch-on timing.

### **2.2 Deep Dive into Sociological to Physical Translation**

The mapping provided in the crosswalk above requires nuanced implementation logic. When integrating this taxonomy into EnergyPlus at a 30-minute resolution, the activity states must act as probability multipliers rather than binary on/off switches. If an occupant enters the "10. Passive leisure" state, the schedule fraction for the TV/Entertainment object does not automatically become 1.0. Instead, the raw 30-minute activity flag increases the probability density function for the television's operation. The calibration scalar (discussed in Section 4\) is then applied to ensure that the television only turns on frequently enough to match annual energy targets.  
Furthermore, lighting requires a tightly coupled, dynamically responsive methodology. As demonstrated by the models of Richardson et al. and Widén et al., residential lighting is rarely mapped to a single, specific activity.19 Instead, lighting demand is a function of *any* active, in-home occupancy (combining Categories 1, 2, 3, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, and 14). However, occupancy merely creates the *need* for visual acuity; the actual electrical load is dynamically modulated by external solar irradiance.6  
In an EnergyPlus context, the aggregated active occupancy profile should serve as the baseline Schedule:Compact indicating the maximum possible lighting demand. However, the model must utilize the Daylighting:Controls object within the thermal zones. This allows the simulation engine to dynamically dim or completely switch off the physical Lights objects based on the available lux levels penetrating the fenestration. Thus, if an occupant is engaged in "1. Work" at 13:00 on a clear summer day, the activity state is positive, but the electrical lighting load remains near zero due to daylight harvesting. If the same activity occurs at 21:00, the electrical load spikes to fulfill the requirement.  
The distinction between active leisure and passive leisure is also critical for accurate thermal modeling. "11. Active leisure" implies a physical exertion that dramatically increases the human body's metabolic rate. While it maps to "NO active load" electrically, the EnergyPlus People object must receive an updated schedule reflecting a shift from a seated metabolic rate (approx. 115 W/person) to a heavy exercise rate (approx. 400 W/person). This shift vastly increases the latent heat fraction, releasing moisture into the air and increasing the dehumidification load on the HVAC system, demonstrating how time-use data impacts building physics beyond simple plug loads.

## **3\. Managing Activity-Independent and Always-On Loads**

A critical pitfall in bottom-up residential demand modeling is the assumption that electricity consumption perfectly and exclusively mirrors occupant behavior. If a modeler strictly relies on the 14-category time-use taxonomy to dictate all loads, the dwelling will erroneously consume zero energy when occupants log "5. Sleep," "12. Community/volunteer," or "13. Travel." In reality, a substantial fraction of a modern dwelling's energy demand operates completely independently of human time-use activities. These continuous loads consist of "always-on" phantom/standby loads and thermostatically controlled appliance cycles.29

### **3.1 Always-On and Standby Power Demand**

Electrical power consumed by products when they are ostensibly switched off or resting in a standby mode is an increasingly massive contributor to residential base load profiles.31 According to extensive monitoring by the U.S. Department of Energy and the Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory (LBNL), standby power—also referred to colloquially as idle load, energy vampires, or phantom load—accounts for a staggering 5% to 10% of total residential energy use.30  
In North American households, this standby load typically ranges from a continuous draw of 50 W to well over 100 W, translating to an average annual consumption capable of costing households upwards of $100 to $183 per year.30 These standby loads originate from a proliferation of continuously powered internal network devices (routers, modems), smart home hubs, cable set-top boxes, digital video recorders (DVRs), and major appliances featuring digital displays or awaiting remote activation signals.30  
Because these devices consume electricity 24 hours a day, 7 days a week, their operation is completely decoupled from the 14-category time-use taxonomy.35 In an EnergyPlus model, standby power must never be modulated by occupant activity. Instead, it must be represented as a permanent, flat fractional schedule (with a constant value of 1.0) applied to a dedicated ElectricEquipment object representing the dwelling's irreducible baseline plug load. This modeling strategy ensures continuous internal heat gain calculation and base electrical demand generation, even when the time-use survey indicates the home is entirely vacant or the occupants are asleep. Failure to include this base load will result in severe under-predictions of annual electricity consumption and inaccurate winter heating load offsets.

### **3.2 Thermostatically Controlled Appliances (Refrigeration)**

Cold appliances, specifically refrigerators and stand-alone freezers, represent the second major category of activity-independent loads. While an occupant may physically interact with a refrigerator during "6. Eating & drinking" or "2. Household work & maintenance," the overwhelming majority of the appliance's energy consumption is driven by autonomous thermodynamic cycling designed to maintain strict internal temperature setpoints, regardless of human presence.  
The CREST model provides a robust methodology for handling refrigeration within a stochastic framework.8 A standard refrigeration unit is modeled using an operational duty cycle combined with a thermostat deadband delay.8 For example, a modeled chest freezer consuming 271 kWh/year operates by drawing 190 W for 14 minutes. This active compressor phase is followed by a mandatory 56-minute delay period where the compressor remains off, representing the thermal deadband as the internal temperature slowly rises.8 This highly regular pattern forces the appliance to cycle approximately 6,116 times per year.8  
When constructing the 30-minute EnergyPlus schedule required by the user, refrigerators and freezers must be modeled as cyclical, continuous elements that ignore the 14-category behavioral data. However, a significant temporal resolution conflict arises: 30-minute intervals are too coarse to capture a 14-minute compressor spike accurately. Consequently, the refrigeration schedule in a 30-minute model must be mathematically smoothed. The refrigerator should be represented as an averaged continuous fractional load (e.g., ![][image3] continuous equivalent). Alternatively, if highly accurate thermal modeling is required, explicit internal zone simulation can be used, where the refrigerator is modeled as an actual thermodynamic zone rejecting heat into the kitchen, though this drastically increases computational overhead. Crucially, whichever abstraction is chosen, this load remains entirely independent of the time-use taxonomy.

## **4\. Calibrating and Anchoring to National Statistics (NRCan SHEU 2019\)**

A stochastic time-use model, no matter how highly resolved or meticulously crosswalked, is mathematically unconstrained unless it is anchored to empirical macroeconomic data. Without calibration, the probabilistic accumulation of thousands of minute switch-on events may drift, yielding annual totals that wildly diverge from reality. To ensure validity, the generated 30-minute load profiles must be forcefully anchored to the 2019 Survey of Household Energy Use (SHEU) provided by Natural Resources Canada (NRCan).37

### **4.1 Overview of SHEU 2019 and the NEUD**

The 2019 SHEU offers a comprehensive, deeply granular overview of the energy use characteristics of roughly 15 million Canadian households.37 In 2019, the Canadian residential sector consumed approximately 1.5 billion gigajoules (GJ) of energy to power activities inside the home.37 The survey breaks down this massive consumption by fuel type, dwelling characteristics, appliances, lighting, and geographic region.  
To properly anchor a residential EnergyPlus model, the modeler must reference specific end-use tables from the SHEU and the associated Comprehensive Energy Use Database (housed within the National Energy Use Database, NEUD):

* **Regional and Dwelling Baselines:** SHEU provides critical macro-level energy intensity figures. In 2019, the average household consumed just under 100 GJ of energy, with the specific average energy intensity for electricity in Canada resting at 39.8 GJ per household.37 However, relying on a national average introduces severe errors, as variance is extreme. Alberta exhibited the highest energy intensity at 124.8 GJ per household, while the Atlantic provinces operated much more efficiently at 85.2 GJ per household.37 Furthermore, dwelling type dictates load: single detached homes averaged 122.8 GJ per household, whereas high-rise apartments consumed a fraction of that at 39.3 GJ per household.37 The base model must be calibrated to the specific region and geometry it represents.  
* **Section 10 – Household Appliances:** To anchor the appliance crosswalks, Tables 10.1a through 10.9b provide exact saturation data for refrigerators, freezers, cooking appliances, dishwashers, clothes washers, clothes dryers, and dehumidifiers, broken down by region and household size.39  
* **Section 11 – Home Electronics:** To anchor the "Work," "Education," and "Passive leisure" categories, Tables 11.1a through 11.10b provide usage and penetration data for televisions, computers, monitors, sound systems, and smart network devices.39  
* **Section 12 – Lighting:** To anchor the active occupancy lighting loads, Tables 12.1a through 12.2b outline the penetration of LED versus incandescent bulbs, noting that 9.9 million households utilized LEDs in 2019, representing a massive shift in baseline lighting wattages.37

### **4.2 Establishing Target Values (Annual kWh)**

According to the NEUD Comprehensive Energy Use Database (specifically *Table 2: Secondary Energy Use and GHG Emissions by End-Use*), the total residential energy use in Canada in 2019 was approximately 1,473.3 PJ to 1,481.5 PJ.38 Drilling down into the specific electrical and behavioral end-uses required to anchor the average Canadian household model:

* **Appliances:** In 2019, residential appliances in Canada consumed 199.1 PJ nationally.40 On an individual household basis, an average set of major appliances purchased in recent years consumes roughly 2,299 kWh to 2,700 kWh per year.41 This figure forms the absolute target for the combined energy output of the appliance mapping generated in Section 2\.  
* **Lighting:** National residential lighting demand accounted for 50.2 PJ in 2019\.40

## **5\. Co-Presence and Household-Size Scaling**

A major complexity in utilizing time-use data for whole-house building energy modeling is managing multi-occupant dynamics. When modeling a household with multiple residents (e.g., a family of four), the electricity demand does not scale linearly with the number of occupants. Simply adding the demand profiles of four individuals together results in massive overestimations of energy use. Scaling mechanisms must account for "economies of scale" inherent to shared domestic appliances and the probabilistic overlap of co-presence.46

### **5.1 Economies of Scale in Household Appliances**

In econometric and sociological studies of household consumption, the Barten model and established equivalence scales dictate that certain goods within a household are public (shared) while others are private (individual).48 This translates directly to appliance utilization and building physics:

* **Shared Appliances (Sub-linear scaling):** Space heating, ambient lighting, and televisions represent shared utilities.50 If three occupants are simultaneously engaged in "10. Passive leisure" in the living room, they share a single television and a single set of overhead light fixtures. The energy consumption of these devices does not triple. Rather, the per-capita electricity consumption decreases drastically due to shared resource utilization.46 Similarly, refrigerator energy consumption is completely independent of household size, acting as a fixed overhead load regardless of occupancy.  
* **Individual Appliances (Linear scaling):** Conversely, personal devices scale almost linearly with household size. The use of laptop computers for "1. Work," specific mobile charging devices, and hot water usage linked to "7. Personal care" (e.g., individual showers) multiply in direct proportion to the number of active occupants.48

## **6\. Methodological Pitfalls and Validation Approaches**

While activity-driven stochastic modeling represents the vanguard of building energy simulation, transitioning from a sociological 14-category time-use dataset to an explicit, functional EnergyPlus schedule is fraught with methodological pitfalls. Establishing a rigorous validation framework is essential to ensure the reliability of the thermal and electrical output.

### **6.1 Critical Pitfalls in Activity-Driven Scheduling**

**1\. The Resolution Gap and Transient Masking:** A primary structural limitation of the user's premise is the reliance on a **30-minute time-series**. Empirical research, particularly the foundational data underlying the CREST model, emphasizes the absolute necessity of 1-minute temporal resolution for capturing domestic electrical loads.1 High-draw residential appliances such as kettles, microwaves, and toasters exhibit massive power spikes (ranging from 1000 W to over 3000 W) that last for only 2 to 5 minutes.22  
Averaging these high-intensity, short-duration events over a 30-minute simulation block artificially flattens the load curve. For example, a microwave used for 3 minutes at 1000 W outputs a continuous 100 W load when averaged over 30 minutes. While this preserves the total energy (kWh) allowing the EnergyPlus internal heat gain calculations to remain thermally accurate over the month, it entirely misrepresents the peak power demand (kW). This resolution gap is catastrophic if the EnergyPlus output is subsequently used to size residential battery storage systems, evaluate electrical panel capacity, or study grid-edge distribution network dynamics, as the simulation will blind the modeler to the true demand spikes.  
**2\. Over-Coincidence and Deterministic Rigidity:** A second major pitfall is the assumption that an activity mathematically guarantees appliance usage, leading to deterministic rigidity. If the simulation logic dictates that the oven turns on *every single time* "6. Eating & drinking" is logged by an occupant, the simulation will massively over-predict convective and radiant thermal cooking loads, forcing the EnergyPlus HVAC system to artificially over-cool the kitchen zone.8 The mapping must strictly remain probabilistic, utilizing the calibration scalars discussed in Section 4.3 to moderate the frequency of actual equipment operation.  
**3\. Ignoring Thermostatic Deadbands in Favor of Behavior:** As established in Section 3, attempting to force refrigerator, freezer, or domestic hot water tank operational cycles to align strictly with occupant "Household work" or "Eating" activities violates the basic laws of thermodynamics.13 Thermal deadbands, recovery times, and standby losses must always override behavioral triggers.

### **6.2 Validation Approaches for Stochastic Load Models**

To guarantee the fidelity of the generated 30-minute schedules prior to their implementation in EnergyPlus, rigorous validation protocols must be executed against empirical metered data. Relying on the calibration scalar to hit the SHEU 2019 annual total is insufficient, as the total volume of energy can be correct while the shape of the load curve is entirely wrong.  
**Quantitative Profile Characterization:**  
Validation must assess the transient *shape* and *variance* of the diurnal curve.

* **Dynamic Time Warping (DTW):** DTW is a highly effective, advanced algorithmic technique used to measure the similarity between two temporal sequences that may vary in speed or timing.22 By utilizing a normalized DTW-distance matrix, the generated 30-minute stochastic schedules can be compared against actual metered residential appliance profiles to evaluate the model's fidelity in capturing realistic peak timing and load duration.22  
* **Median Difference Test (MDT):** This statistical method is employed to formally validate profile characterization, ensuring that the median behavior of the synthetic load closely aligns with observed empirical distributions across specific critical time intervals, such as morning and evening peaks.22

**Load Coincidence and Diversity Factors:** A hallmark of a mathematically sound stochastic model is its ability to accurately reflect load coincidence when multiple schedules are aggregated.14 When modeling a single, isolated household, the load profile should be highly volatile and jagged. However, if the schedules of 50 distinct EnergyPlus dwelling models are aggregated together, the resultant profile should smooth out dramatically, demonstrating the central limit theorem and resembling a standard regional utility load curve.17 Validating the diversity factor—defined as the ratio of the sum of the individual maximum demands to the maximum demand of the entire aggregated system—against empirical Canadian utility data ensures the activity crosswalk is probabilistically robust across a neighborhood scale.14  
**Statistical Variance Metrics:** In their foundational 2010 work, Widén and Wäckelgård validated their Markov-chain models by directly comparing the standard deviations of their synthetic data against real metered data.20 Valid stochastic models generate standard deviations that differ from high-resolution metered data by minimal margins (e.g., an error margin of less than 4%).20 Ensuring the variance of the generated 30-minute schedule matches the variance of real-world homes guarantees that the activity-to-appliance mapping has successfully captured the true, unpredictable stochastic nature of human domestic behavior, resulting in an EnergyPlus model that behaves like a lived-in home rather than a deterministic machine.
