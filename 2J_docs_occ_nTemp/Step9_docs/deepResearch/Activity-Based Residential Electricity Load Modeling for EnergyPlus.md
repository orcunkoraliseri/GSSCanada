# Activity-Based Residential Electricity Load Modeling for EnergyPlus: A Structured Synthesis Anchored to NRCan SHEU 2019

## TL;DR
- The established activity-based bottom-up models (Richardson/CREST, Widén & Wäckelgård, Fischer's synPRO, Muratori, Baetens's StROBe, Yamaguchi/Shimoda's TREES) all share one architecture: a Markov-chain active-occupancy generator → activity-conditioned appliance switch-on probabilities → rated-power/measured-trace assignment → 1–10 minute load profiles. This maps cleanly onto a 30-minute Quebec model using a 14-category GSS taxonomy with per-end-use scalar calibration to NRCan/Hydro-Québec totals.
- The defensible calibration strategy is two-tier: hold always-on baseload (fridge, freezer, networking, standby) as constant fractional EnergyPlus schedules, then scale the activity-driven plug/lighting schedules so each simulated end-use's annual kWh matches its SHEU/Hydro-Québec target via a per-category correction factor. For Quebec, the appliances+lighting block your activity model must reproduce is ~23% of the ~17,750 kWh/yr household total (~4,000 kWh/yr).
- The dominant pitfalls are under-represented seasonality (time-use data don't capture winter indoor intensification), single-household variance (use stochastic ensembles, not one run), and rare high-power events (EV, hot tub, pool) that must be added as explicit separate schedules rather than emerging from time-use. Validate on two axes: annual energy (SHEU/Hydro-Québec) and load shape (peak timing, diversity factor) against measured Canadian data (Saldanha & Beausoleil-Morrison's 12-house dataset).

## Key Findings

1. **A single canonical architecture dominates the field.** Richardson, Thomson, Infield & Clifford (2010) established the template now used (with variations) by virtually all activity-based residential electricity models: simulate *active occupancy* (people home AND awake) via a first-order Markov chain calibrated to a national Time-Use Survey, then for each appliance compute a switch-on probability each timestep that is the product of (a) a calendar/activity-profile weighting and (b) a calibration constant tuned so mean annual use matches statistics. Power is assigned from rated/measured values, including distinct standby and active states. The paper is cited 1,051 times (Semantic Scholar index) and was validated against a full year of recorded demand "within 22 dwellings in the East Midlands, UK."

2. **Co-presence is handled non-linearly, not linearly.** The Richardson/CREST family increases appliance-use likelihood with the number of active occupants, but *non-linearly* (sub-linearly), which is the explicit mechanism that represents appliance sharing (one TV serves several people). This is the single most important structural choice for household-size scaling.

3. **SHEU 2019 gives you the anchor totals but not a native activity crosswalk.** SHEU 2019 provides end-use shares (space heating 64%, water heating 17%, appliances 14%, lighting 4%, space cooling 2% of residential energy), provincial electricity intensity (Quebec 63.9 GJ/household ≈ 17,750 kWh/yr — the highest in Canada and more than double Alberta), dwelling-type breakdowns, and appliance/electronics ownership and usage-hour distributions. NRCan's Comprehensive Energy Use Database supplies the appliance unit energy consumption (UEC) values needed for the bottom-up build.

4. **A Canadian-calibrated analogue already exists.** The Concordia-relevant "Stochastic bottom-up load profile generator for Canadian households' electricity demand" (Building and Environment, 2023) calibrates the Richardson/Widén approach to the Canadian Time-Use Survey, appliance ownership and energy statistics — directly relevant precedent. Measured validation data exist (Saldanha & Beausoleil-Morrison 2012, 12 Canadian houses at 1-minute resolution; Johnson & Beausoleil-Morrison 2017, 23 houses).

## Details

### TOPIC 1 — Established activity-based / bottom-up residential electricity demand models

**Richardson, Thomson, Infield & Clifford (2010).** *Domestic electricity use: A high-resolution energy demand model.* Energy and Buildings, 42(10), 1878–1887. DOI: 10.1016/j.enbuild.2010.05.023. Open access; 1,051 citations.
- (a) Activity/occupancy data source: UK 2000 Time Use Survey (Ipsos-RSL / ONS). Occupancy is generated by the companion Richardson, Thomson & Infield (2008) Markov-chain active-occupancy model.
- (b) Probability mechanism: Each appliance has an associated *activity profile* describing how the probability of the relevant activity varies through the day. Each timestep, an appliance not currently on may switch on with a probability = (activity probability for the relevant active-occupancy level) × (a calibration scalar). The scalar is set so the modelled mean annual energy per appliance matches published average annual consumption. Appliance ownership is set stochastically from saturation/penetration statistics.
- (c) Power assignment: Each appliance has a rated active power and a standby power; some appliances (washing machine, dishwasher) use a multi-phase power signature. Once switched on, the appliance runs for a duration drawn from a mean-duration distribution.
- (d) Temporal resolution: 1 minute, 24 h, weekday/weekend distinction.
- (e) Calibration: Per-appliance calibration scalar to hit mean annual energy; validated against 1 year of measured demand in 22 East Midlands dwellings, showing similar statistical characteristics (load-duration, diversity).

Generates 1-minute activity AND electricity data. Household-size effect is captured because activities are simulated per-member and shared appliances are not double-counted.

- Widén, J., Molin, A., & Ellegård, K. (2012). *Models of domestic occupancy, activities and energy use based on time-use data.* Journal of Building Performance Simulation, 5(1), 27–44. DOI: 10.1080/19401493.2010.532569.

**CREST Demand Model (Loughborough University).** The productized, open-source successor to the Richardson model, extended to integrated thermal-electrical.
- McKenna, E., & Thomson, M. (2016). *High-resolution stochastic integrated thermal–electrical domestic demand model.* Applied Energy, 165, 445–461. DOI: 10.1016/j.apenergy.2015.12.089. Open access. (Integrates appliances, lighting, PV with an updated occupancy model, low-order building thermal model, DHW, thermostat/timer controls and gas. Bottom-up activity-based, stochastic, calibrated and validated, free open-source.)
- McKenna, E., Krawczynski, M., & Thomson, M. (2015). *Four-state domestic building occupancy model for energy demand simulations.* Energy and Buildings, 96, 30–39. DOI: 10.1016/j.enbuild.2015.03.013. Open access. (Extends two-state active-occupancy into four states — separating absent/present from active/inactive, i.e. distinguishing sleep from absence; first-order Markov chain; corrects under-representation of 24-h occupancy; represents naturally correlated occupancy in multi-occupant dwellings.)

- Link from time-use to appliance use: identical to Richardson 2010 — active occupancy + per-appliance activity profiles → switch-on probability → calibrated mean use; lighting uses irradiance + effective (shared) occupancy.

### TOPIC 2 — Activity-to-end-use crosswalk for the 14-category GSS taxonomy

See the **Activity → End-Use Crosswalk Table** (Output (a) below). Design principles: (i) Lighting and HVAC are governed by *presence/active occupancy* and daylight/temperature, not by specific activity, so they are triggered across nearly all at-home activities and are handled by the lighting/HVAC sub-models, not the discrete activity crosswalk. (ii) Travel, Purchasing, and Community/volunteer almost always imply the occupant is *away*, so they trigger zero active plug load (only baseload continues). (iii) Cooking is triggered by Eating & drinking and by Household work. (iv) Shared "room" devices (TV, stereo) are triggered by the first occupant in passive/active leisure and socializing; personal devices (laptop, phone, personal care) scale per-person.

Typical power ratings (sources: ENERGY STAR / NRCan UEC, Richardson 2010 appliance set, CREST documentation, manufacturer/utility ranges):
- Microwave 900–1,500 W (short bursts); electric range/oven element 1,200–3,000 W; cooktop element ~1,500–2,000 W; kettle ~3,000 W; toaster ~1,000 W.
- Dishwasher 1,200–2,400 W active (plus hot-water draw); ~71 kWh/yr (new, NRCan 2019).
- Clothes washer 350–500 W; ~25 kWh/yr (new). Clothes dryer 1,800–5,000 W; ~591 kWh/yr (new).
- Television (flat-screen LED) ~30–120 W; set-top/devices used with TV add load; desktop CPU ~120 W active / monitor ~30–150 W; laptop ~50 W.
- Personal care: hair dryer 1,200–1,875 W; iron 1,000–1,800 W (short bursts).
- Refrigerator ~471 kWh/yr new (NRCan 2019), running intermittently at ~100–150 W compressor draw; freezer ~324 kWh/yr new.

### TOPIC 3 — Always-on / activity-independent loads

Baseload should be modelled *separately* from the activity-driven schedules, as a constant (or near-constant) fractional EnergyPlus schedule for the relevant ElectricEquipment objects — e.g., a fridge/freezer object with a schedule fraction near 1.0 (modulated only by a duty-cycle if you wish to emulate compressor cycling), and a "standby/networking" object held flat at its average wattage. This prevents the activity model from spuriously zeroing out loads that physically run 24/7.

Magnitudes and shares (best available, UK/EU/IEA where Canadian is thin):
- **UK Household Electricity Survey (DECC/DEFRA, "Further Analysis of the Household Electricity Survey," 2014):** average overnight **baseload ≈ 220 W/household**; evening peak is more than 3× baseload. This is the best-documented measured baseload figure.
- **Standby/phantom aggregate share:** IEA's *Things That Go Blip in the Night* (2001) found a typical US home required ~50 W standby on average ≈ 440 kWh/yr ≈ 5% of residential electricity; LBNL studies place phantom/standby at 5–10% of household electricity. Hydro-Québec's own consumer guidance states the wider range that **"phantom power could be responsible for 5 to 20% of your electricity bills"** — so treat 5–10% as a conservative central estimate and 20% as an upper bound for device-heavy homes.
- **Conversion rule:** each watt of continuous load ≈ **9 kWh/yr** (1 W × 8,766 h).
- **Refrigerator:** ~471 kWh/yr (new, NRCan 2019); stock average higher (older units). **Freezer:** ~324 kWh/yr (new). Almost 4.7 million Canadian households have a second refrigerator (SHEU 2019), a significant always-on add.
- **Networking equipment:** SHEU 2019 reports network devices are "always turned on" in 62% of households and cellphones in 40%; a typical router/modem at ~10–12 W continuous ≈ 88–105 kWh/yr. **Set-top boxes:** LBNL measured ~100 kWh/yr average UEC.
- In the Quebec appliance breakdown, refrigerator + freezer alone are 46% of the "appliances & electronics" slice (Hydro-Québec/NRCan: fridge 33%, freezer 13%), so the always-on cold-appliance component is the single largest activity-independent block.

EnergyPlus representation: assign cold appliances and networking/standby to dedicated `ElectricEquipment` objects with a constant `Schedule:Compact` fraction (e.g., always 1.0 at a design level equal to the average power), so annual kWh equals the SHEU/UEC anchor; everything else is driven by the activity schedule.

### TOPIC 4 — Anchoring/calibrating to NRCan SHEU 2019

**What SHEU 2019 provides:** dwelling characteristics (type: single detached 60%, double/row/duplex 13%, low-rise apt 12%, high-rise apt 10%, mobile 1%); number and use of appliances, electronics and other products; appliance age distributions; lighting bulb-type counts and seasonal usage hours; electronic-device weekly usage-hour distributions (including "always on" categories); energy-efficiency characteristics; and consumption broken down by region/province, dwelling type, income, tenure and heated area. SHEU is paired with the Comprehensive Energy Use Database (Energy Use Data Handbook) which provides end-use energy splits and appliance Unit Energy Consumption (UEC, Table 16).

**Published figures (national, SHEU 2019 / NRCan Trends 2019):**
- Average household total energy intensity: 98.1 GJ/household; electricity intensity 39.8 GJ/household (~11,060 kWh/yr).
- Residential energy by end use: space heating 64%, water heating 17%, appliances 14%, lighting 4%, space cooling 2%.
- Lighting energy: 3.6 GJ/household/yr (down 25% from 4.7 GJ in 2000).
- New-appliance UEC (kWh/yr): refrigerator 471, freezer 324, dishwasher 71, clothes washer 25, clothes dryer 591, range 548.
- National appliance electricity by appliance (2019, PJ): refrigerator 7.3, freezer 1.7, dishwasher 0.5, clothes washer 0.4, dryer 7.0, range 8.0, other appliances/electronics 21.1; total electric appliances ≈ 46.0 PJ; appliance energy intensity ≈ 10.4 GJ/household. Per-household electric-appliance total ≈ **~2,735 kWh/yr**.

**Quebec-specific (the relevant context):**
- Quebec electricity intensity: **63.9 GJ/household ≈ 17,750 kWh/yr** (SHEU 2019), the highest in Canada (Quebec consumed 40% of national residential electricity); driven by near-universal electric space and water heating (electric baseboards dominate, used by ~66% of Quebec households per SHEU 2011).
- **Hydro-Québec end-use split**, confirmed verbatim for "the average household that uses electric heating": **54% heating and air-conditioning, 20% hot water, 18% appliances and electronics, 5% lighting, 3% other.** Within the 18% appliances slice (Hydro-Québec, sourced to Natural Resources Canada): refrigerator 33%, dryer 18%, other [TVs, DVD players, radios, computers] 16%, range 15%, freezer 13%, dishwasher 3% (excl. hot water), washer 2% (excl. hot water). Within the 20% hot-water slice (source: Canadian Building Energy End-Use Data and Analysis Centre): faucets 34%, showers 25%, baths 17%, laundry 15%, dishwashing 4%, leaks 5%. The "3% other" category is explicitly stated by Hydro-Québec to include "pool equipment, a sump pump, woodworking tools, etc." and "does not reflect the actual consumption of a household with a pool, which is much higher than 3%."
- Applying these to ~17,750 kWh/yr: heating+AC ≈ 9,585 kWh, water heating ≈ 3,550 kWh, appliances/electronics ≈ 3,195 kWh, lighting ≈ 888 kWh, other ≈ 533 kWh.

**Appliance + lighting share of total household electricity (excluding space heating/cooling and water heating):** Nationally, appliances (14%) + lighting (4%) = 18% of total residential *energy* (all fuels, including heating). Once space heating, cooling and water heating are excluded, the appliance+lighting block is essentially the entire remaining non-thermal electricity. For Quebec specifically, appliances/electronics (18%) + lighting (5%) = **~23% of household electricity** (the non-thermal remainder), i.e. ~4,080 kWh/yr — the portion your activity-driven model is actually responsible for reproducing.

**Calibration/normalization procedure (recommended):**
1. Build the bottom-up model with appliance ownership from SHEU 2019 saturation and rated powers/durations from UEC and the literature.
2. Run a stochastic ensemble (many households × full year) to obtain simulated annual kWh per end-use category.
3. For each end-use category *i*, compute a scalar correction factor f_i = (SHEU/Hydro-Québec target kWh_i) / (simulated kWh_i).
4. Apply f_i to the switch-on probability calibration constant (Richardson-style) or to the design-level wattage of the corresponding EnergyPlus ElectricEquipment object, then re-run and iterate until convergence.
5. Hold baseload categories fixed (Topic 3) and let activity-driven categories absorb the remaining target.

This per-end-use scalar approach is exactly how Richardson 2010 calibrates each appliance (scalar to match mean annual energy) and how the Canadian generators (Armstrong 2009; the 2023 Canadian generator) anchor to NRCan statistics; broader UBEM calibration literature (smart-meter pattern-matching) validates the normalization-to-measured-totals method.

### TOPIC 5 — Co-presence and household-size scaling

- **Shared/room devices (TV, stereo, room lighting):** used by the first active occupant; the marginal effect of additional co-present occupants is minimal. Richardson (2008/2010) and CREST capture this by making appliance-use likelihood increase *non-linearly (sub-linearly)* with the number of active occupants — explicitly described as the mechanism that lets "this sharing of appliances be taken into account." The lighting model uses "effective occupancy" so occupants sharing a room share the light.
- **Per-person devices (laptops, phones, personal care):** scale approximately linearly with the number of occupants, since each person uses their own. Widén & Wäckelgård (2010) and Fischer (2015) achieve this by simulating activities per household member and assigning per-member appliances, while allowing shared appliances not to be double-counted.
- **Cooking:** scales sub-linearly — one cooking event serves multiple people — consistent with the non-linear active-occupancy weighting.
- **Richardson/CREST handling:** the active-occupancy model outputs the *number* of active occupants; appliance switch-on probability rises with that number but less than proportionally, encoding sharing. Widén & Wäckelgård handle household size by per-member activity simulation, so larger households show higher but sub-proportional per-capita appliance use.
- **Canadian-specific data:** SHEU 2019 / NRCan Trends shows 2.5 people per household (2019) and reports per-household (not per-capita) consumption; the well-established empirical pattern is that per-capita residential energy *declines* as household size grows (economies of scale in shared appliances, lighting and space), which the non-linear co-presence weighting reproduces. Quebec's average household is small (smallest average heated area nationally, 158.1 m²); two-member households are most common nationally (35%), one-member 31%.

### TOPIC 6 — Pitfalls and validation approaches

- **Seasonality / "time-use rebound":** Activity-based models tend to *underestimate seasonal variation* because time-use surveys don't capture that people stay in more in winter. Richardson 2010 itself notes the resulting winter increase in general appliance use is not represented (only lighting picks up the seasonal signal via irradiance). A German replication explicitly found the Richardson model underestimates seasonal trends. Mitigation: apply seasonal probability sets (as Fischer's synPRO does) and seasonality factors per appliance type (as the UK HES analysis derived).
- **Appliance ownership assumptions:** Results are highly sensitive to saturation/penetration rates; use SHEU 2019 ownership (e.g., 4.7M second refrigerators, TV in 14.6M households, computer/tablet in 13.8M).
- **Stochastic diversity vs. single household:** A single simulated household is highly stochastic and unrepresentative; run a stochastic *ensemble* and judge against aggregate/diversity statistics. Diversity (non-coincidence of peaks) is the property these models exist to reproduce; Baetens & Saelens (2016) show regression-to-the-mean as you aggregate to feeder scale.
- **Rare high-power events (EV charging, hot tub, pool pump):** These do not emerge naturally from time-use and must be added as explicit separate schedules/objects. SHEU 2019: ~655k households (4%) own an electric/hybrid vehicle; Hydro-Québec's "3% other" slice includes pool equipment and "does not reflect the actual consumption of a household with a pool, which is much higher than 3%." Treat EV and pool/hot-tub as deterministic or separately-stochastic add-on loads.
- **Lighting / daylight interaction:** Lighting must be coupled to daylight availability (irradiance) AND active occupancy (Richardson 2009; CREST irradiance database). In EnergyPlus, reconcile this with the daylighting controls so you don't double-count.
- **Validation approaches:** (1) Annual-energy anchoring to SHEU/Hydro-Québec totals per end use; (2) load-shape metrics — morning/evening peak timing, peak magnitude, load-duration curves, and **diversity/coincidence factor**; (3) comparison to measured smart-meter / sub-metered data. Richardson 2010 validated against 22 UK dwellings (1 year); McKenna & Thomson 2016 validated the integrated model; Widén validated against Swedish end-use measurements. **For Canada/Quebec, validate against Saldanha & Beausoleil-Morrison (2012), "Measured end-use electric load profiles for 12 Canadian houses at high temporal resolution," Energy and Buildings, 49, 519–530, DOI: 10.1016/j.enbuild.2012.02.013, and Johnson & Beausoleil-Morrison (2017), Applied Thermal Engineering, 114, 1449–1456 (23 houses, 1-minute), both with downloadable data.**

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt the Richardson/CREST architecture but re-fit to Canadian inputs.** Use the four-state McKenna occupancy model (to separate sleep from absence — important for your Travel/Purchasing/Community "absent" categories and for HVAC), the Richardson appliance switch-on mechanism, and the Richardson 2009 lighting sub-model. Precedent and partial code exist in the 2023 Canadian generator and Armstrong (2009).
2. **Implement the two-tier load split in EnergyPlus.** One set of `ElectricEquipment` objects with constant fractional schedules for always-on baseload (fridge, freezer, networking/standby), sized to NRCan UEC; a second set driven by the 30-minute activity-derived schedules for cooking, dishwashing, laundry, entertainment, computing and personal care.
3. **Calibrate per end-use with scalar correction factors** to Hydro-Québec's Quebec split (heating+AC 54%, water 20%, appliances 18%, lighting 5%, other 3% of ~17,750 kWh/yr), iterating on the activity-driven categories while holding baseload fixed. Success benchmark: each simulated end-use within ±10% of its anchor, and total non-thermal electricity ≈ 4,000–4,100 kWh/yr (Quebec appliances+lighting).
4. **Use the GSS crosswalk table below**, treating Travel, Purchasing and Community/volunteer as occupant-absent (baseload only), and applying non-linear co-presence weighting for shared devices and per-person scaling for personal devices.
5. **Add EV and pool/hot-tub as explicit separate loads** rather than expecting them from time-use; gate EV charging on the 4% ownership rate (higher if your sample is high-income).
6. **Validate on two axes**: annual energy (SHEU/Hydro-Québec) AND load shape (peak timing + diversity factor) against the Saldanha/Beausoleil-Morrison Canadian datasets. **Thresholds that change the approach:** if simulated seasonal amplitude is materially below measured, introduce seasonal probability sets (Fischer approach); if single-run variance dominates, increase the stochastic ensemble size until aggregate metrics stabilize.

## Caveats
- SHEU 2019 reports energy by source and by end use largely in GJ and as *shares*; precise per-household kWh by individual appliance for Quebec specifically is **derived** (province total × Hydro-Québec percentage split) rather than directly published, and should be treated as an estimate. The Hydro-Québec percentage breakdown applies to households heating with electricity (the Quebec norm) and bundles space heating with air-conditioning (54%), so cooling cannot be cleanly separated from that figure.
- Several appliance wattage values are typical ranges from utility/manufacturer compilations, not peer-reviewed; use NRCan UEC for annual-energy anchoring and reserve instantaneous wattages for load-shape/EnergyPlus design-level inputs.
- The standby/networking share figures derive from US/IEA/LBNL sources and older surveys; Canadian standby data are sparse, and Hydro-Québec's own consumer figure (5–20%) is wider than the 5–10% measured central estimate. Some widely cited IEA network-standby totals (46 TWh by 2025; 300 TWh by 2030) are *projections*, not measurements.
- The NRCan online appliance table has labeling quirks; confirm stock-average UEC (Table 16) via the downloadable Excel handbook before finalizing per-appliance anchors.
- The "Stochastic bottom-up load profile generator for Canadian households" (2023) DOI and the exact GSS 14-category labels should be re-verified against the primary publication and Statistics Canada GSS time-use documentation, as taxonomy wording varies between GSS cycles.
