# Modern Deep Structured Prediction: A Primer for Transformer Practitioners Facing "Correct Marginals, Wrong Joint"

## TL;DR
- Your diagnosis is textbook structured-prediction failure: independent per-slot cross-entropy and BCE losses are calibrated to recover correct one-dimensional marginals but provide *zero* gradient signal about the joint distribution, so mutual exclusivity, (activity, AT_HOME) coherence, and temporal persistence are precisely the failure modes the theory predicts. The right reading list is, in order, Lafferty–McCallum–Pereira 2001 (CRF), LeCun et al. 2006 (EBM tutorial), Ma & Hovy 2016 (BiLSTM-CNN-CRF), Belanger & McCallum 2016 (SPEN), Grathwohl et al. ICLR 2020 (JEM), and Niculae et al. 2025 *Foundations and Trends in Signal Processing* (Discrete Latent Structure in Neural Networks).
- For your specific problem, the highest-leverage and lowest-risk intervention is **(i) merge AT_HOME and activity into a single joint categorical head with a structurally-restricted vocabulary** (this kills the mutual-exclusivity and (activity, AT_HOME) coherence problems by construction), then **(ii) add a linear-chain CRF layer on top of the Transformer** (this gives a tractable, exact treatment of temporal persistence). Auxiliary pairwise/transition penalty losses are the worst pragmatic option because they routinely produce the head-collapse pathology you already observed; they should be a last resort and, if used, must be calibrated with Kendall–Gal–Cipolla 2018 uncertainty weighting or GradNorm.
- Empirically, post-Transformer gains from explicit structure are real but *modest and task-dependent*: on the canonical CoNLL-2003 English NER benchmark, adding a CRF to BERT yields essentially noise-level deltas (≈+0.1 F1), but on lower-resource and constraint-heavy regimes (Portuguese HAREM I, smart-home CASAS, joint intent-slot) the CRF or semi-Markov CRF still adds 1.4–3 F1 points — and *segmentation* (semi-Markov) gains are larger than transition-only gains. The structured-prediction literature is not dead post-Transformer; it has narrowed to where structure is hard, low-data, or constraint-explicit, which is exactly your regime.

## Key Findings

1. **Pointwise losses are provably blind to joint structure.** Independent cross-entropy and BCE factorize the loss across positions and labels; the gradient at position *t* depends only on the per-position prediction and target, so the model has no training signal about pairwise or higher-order co-occurrence beyond what the encoder happens to learn implicitly through context. Structured losses fix this by scoring *whole outputs*.
2. **Energy-based models (EBMs) are the right unifying lens.** LeCun et al. 2006 frame CRFs, max-margin Markov nets, SPENs and JEM as special cases of: define a scalar energy E(x, y) over inputs and full outputs, train so the data has lower energy than alternatives, decode by argmin/argmax over Y. This frame makes "joint" first-class.
3. **Differentiable structured inference is now a solved engineering problem for chain structures.** Linear-chain CRFs admit exact O(T·K²) forward-backward and Viterbi; SparseMAP, Gumbel-softmax / Concrete, Perturb-and-MAP, and SPIGOT provide gradient estimators for richer structures, but for your per-time-slot activity+AT_HOME problem you do not need them — a CRF is exact.
4. **Self-supervised pretraining is a *partial* structural prior, not a substitute.** Masked modeling implicitly trains conditional joint distributions, but a pointwise fine-tuning objective will demonstrably wash out joint coherence on long-range or rare-structure constraints. This matches your observation.
5. **Auxiliary pairwise/transition penalty losses are pragmatic but fragile.** They almost always cause gradient conflict with the primary loss and are responsible for the head-collapse pathology you saw. Uncertainty weighting (Kendall, Gal & Cipolla, CVPR 2018) and GradNorm (Chen et al., ICML 2018) help, but the CRF-layer approach is theoretically cleaner: it folds the structural constraint into the likelihood instead of fighting it as a regularizer.
6. **Transformer + CRF still helps when it matters.** For canonical English NER on CoNLL-2003 the BERT+CRF delta is essentially zero (a public reproduction reports BertForTokenClassification 92.17 F1 vs BERT+CRF 92.29 F1, +0.12). But Souza, Nogueira & Lotufo (arXiv:1909.10649) on Portuguese HAREM I report that the IOB2 transition filter from a CRF improves F1 by 2.24 and 1.26 points (feature-based and fine-tuning, respectively), and on smart-home CASAS data, Thapa et al. (*Sensors* 20(20):5770, 2020) report that their BiLSTM + Skip-Chain CRF surpasses prior approaches with an average accuracy of more than 93%. The lesson: CRFs help most when (a) the encoder is weak, (b) data is limited, or (c) hard combinatorial constraints exist — all of which describe occupancy modeling more than they describe English NER.

## Details

### 1. The Fundamental Gap: Why Pointwise Losses Fail for Joint Structure

**Formal setup.** Structured prediction posits an output space Y that is not a single label but a *combinatorial object*: a sequence y = (y_1, ..., y_T) with y_t ∈ V, possibly with auxiliary binary labels (your AT_HOME flag is exactly such an output coordinate). A scoring function s_θ: X × Y → ℝ produces a real-valued compatibility, and prediction is

  ŷ = argmax_{y ∈ Y} s_θ(x, y).

The probabilistic instantiation defines p_θ(y | x) = exp(s_θ(x, y)) / Z(x), where Z(x) = Σ_{y' ∈ Y} exp(s_θ(x, y')) is the partition function.

**Why independent CE/BCE fail.** When the score factorizes as s_θ(x, y) = Σ_t s_θ(x, y_t) — i.e., when the per-position logits are independent — the partition function decomposes and the loss becomes Σ_t CE(p_t, y_t*). The gradient at position *t* depends only on (x, y_t*). There is *no term* in the loss that couples y_t with y_{t-1}, with AT_HOME_t, or with y_s for s ≠ t. So the model can only learn coupling through the *encoder's hidden state* — and a Transformer trained pointwise has no incentive to push its representations to produce coherent joints, only to be locally accurate. That is exactly the regime in which marginals match but joints break.

**What structured losses provide.** The negative log-likelihood under a non-factored model has the form L(θ) = −s_θ(x, y*) + log Z(x). The gradient of log Z(x) is an expectation over the *whole* output space and therefore *does* depend on coupling. For chain structures, this is computed exactly by forward-backward. The classical theoretical guarantees are:
- **Fisher consistency** for the structured-hinge and structured-CRF losses (under realizable assumptions): minimizing the surrogate recovers the Bayes-optimal *structured* predictor, not the marginal-optimal one.
- **Calibration** results (Ciliberto et al. 2016; Nowak-Vila et al.) showing that surrogate structured losses upper-bound true structured task losses (Hamming, F1, sequence accuracy).
- **Decomposability**: pointwise losses are decomposable, which is exactly *why* they cannot capture joint structure; structured losses are explicitly non-decomposable.

### 2. Energy-Based Model Formulations

**LeCun, Chopra, Hadsell, Ranzato, Huang. "A Tutorial on Energy-Based Learning." In G. Bakir et al., eds., *Predicting Structured Data*, MIT Press, 2006** (v1.0 dated August 19, 2006). The tutorial introduced the unifying view that *every* supervised learner with a scalar compatibility — CRFs, max-margin Markov nets, structural SVMs, graph transformer networks, contrastive losses, and many manifold learners — is a special case of an energy-based model. Inference is argmin_y E(x, y); learning is shaping E so observed (x, y) have lower energy than other (x, y'). The tutorial catalogues which loss functions (NLL, hinge, perceptron, square-square, contrastive) actually create the necessary energy *gap* between correct and incorrect answers, and which (e.g., naive perceptron loss) collapse.

**Belanger & McCallum. "Structured Prediction Energy Networks." *Proceedings of the 33rd International Conference on Machine Learning* (ICML 2016), PMLR 48: 983–992; arXiv:1511.06350.** SPENs parameterize a deep network E_θ(x, y) directly over relaxed continuous y ∈ [0, 1]^|Y|, and perform inference by gradient descent on y to minimize E_θ. This bypasses the factor-graph framework: any architecture (MLP, attention) can play the role of energy. Training uses a structured hinge (SSVM) loss; the follow-up **Belanger, Yang & McCallum, "End-to-End Learning for SPENs," ICML 2017 (arXiv:1703.05667)** replaces SSVM with end-to-end backpropagation through the unrolled gradient-descent inference, which is more accurate but more expensive. **Tu & Gimpel ("Learning Approximate Inference Networks for Structured Prediction," ICLR 2018; arXiv:1803.03376)** swap the gradient-descent inference for a trained "inference network" — cheaper at test time but introduces an approximation gap.

**Grathwohl, Wang, Jacobsen, Duvenaud, Norouzi, Swersky. "Your Classifier Is Secretly an Energy Based Model and You Should Treat It Like One." ICLR 2020 (arXiv:1912.03263).** Key argument: a standard softmax classifier outputs logits f_θ(x)[y]; these are unnormalized log-conditionals. Define E(x, y) = −f_θ(x)[y] and you immediately get *both* a conditional p(y|x) = softmax(f_θ(x)) (the usual classifier) and a free-energy marginal p(x) ∝ Σ_y exp(f_θ(x)[y]) for free — without modifying the architecture. The paper trains the joint p(x, y) via the standard discriminative cross-entropy loss *plus* a generative log p(x) loss estimated with Stochastic Gradient Langevin Dynamics (SGLD) on x. Quoting the abstract: "energy based training of the joint distribution improves calibration, robustness, and out-of-distribution detection while also enabling our models to generate samples rivaling the quality of recent GAN approaches. Our approach is able to achieve performance rivaling the state-of-the-art in both generative and discriminative learning within one hybrid model." On CIFAR-100 the paper documents that a JEM-trained model is "nearly perfectly calibrated when measured with Expected Calibration Error," while remaining competitive in discriminative accuracy with the baseline (~74.8%). This is the most direct theoretical justification for treating your activity/AT_HOME problem jointly: training the joint pays for itself even when you only care about the conditional.

**Why EBMs naturally handle joint distributions.** The whole point of E(x, y_1, ..., y_T, AT_HOME_1, ..., AT_HOME_T) is that it does not decompose. You can add a term like w · 𝟙[activity_t = "sleeping" ∧ AT_HOME_t = 0] (a forbidden combination) and that term enters the loss for *every* sample, pushing the joint mass off impossible configurations. With pointwise CE/BCE, no analogous term exists.

**Training challenges.** EBM training for sequence outputs requires either:
- *Exact* partition-function computation (only for chain or low-treewidth structures: linear-chain CRF, semi-Markov CRF).
- *Sampling-based contrastive divergence* (Hinton 2002), or its modern incarnations (persistent contrastive divergence, short-run MCMC, SGLD as in JEM, score matching) — these are notoriously brittle for high-dimensional discrete sequences and frequently destabilize training. Grathwohl et al. document this and suggest spectral normalization and replay buffers as mitigations.
- *Inference network approximation* (Tu & Gimpel, SPEN follow-ups) — train a separate network to predict argmin_y E.

**Verdict for your problem**: sequence-length T × |V|^T is too large for naive enumeration, but a *linear-chain* factorization gives you an exact, cheap dynamic-programming partition function and exact gradients. You should use that, not generic EBM training.

### 3. Differentiable Approximations to Combinatorial Losses

**Niculae, Martins, Blondel, Cardie. "SparseMAP: Differentiable Sparse Structured Inference." ICML 2018, PMLR 80: 3799–3808; arXiv:1802.04223.** SparseMAP solves an intermediate problem between MAP (a single argmax structure) and marginal inference (probability over all structures). It returns a *sparse convex combination of a few structures* — typically 2 to 5 — which lives on a face of the marginal polytope (the convex hull of all valid output indicator vectors). Critical properties:
- Computable using only calls to a *MAP oracle* — you do not need marginal inference. This makes it applicable to problems like matchings/assignments where marginalization is #P-hard.
- Gradients are efficiently backpropagable because the solution is sparse.
- Recovers softmax in the unstructured-categorical case and sparsemax (Martins & Astudillo 2016) for the single-variable case.
- The interpretation is that SparseMAP is a structured generalization of sparsemax, sitting between softmax (dense) and argmax (single-structure).

**LP-relaxation losses.** MAP inference for general graphical models is NP-hard; the standard relaxation replaces the marginal polytope M(G) with the *local polytope* L(G) defined by pairwise consistency constraints. Surrogate losses based on LP relaxations (e.g., Meshi et al., Kulesza & Pereira) provide upper bounds on true structured losses with tractable gradients. These are theoretically clean but typically dominated in practice by direct CRF/SparseMAP training for chain structures.

**Papandreou & Yuille. "Perturb-and-MAP Random Fields: Using Discrete Optimization to Learn and Sample from Energy Models." ICCV 2011, pp. 193–200.** The key insight: if you add IID Gumbel-distributed noise to *every* configuration's energy and take the argmax of the perturbed energy, you sample exactly from the Gibbs distribution. For tractable MAP oracles (graph cuts, Viterbi), this turns sampling into optimization. The cost: independent Gumbel noise has |Y| degrees of freedom, so practical implementations use *low-rank* (unary) Gumbel perturbations which only approximate the Gibbs distribution but remain tractable.

**Jang, Gu, Poole. "Categorical Reparameterization with Gumbel-Softmax." ICLR 2017 (arXiv:1611.01144); concurrently Maddison, Mnih, Teh. "The Concrete Distribution: A Continuous Relaxation of Discrete Random Variables." ICLR 2017 (arXiv:1611.00712).** Both papers independently relax categorical argmax: draw Gumbel noise g_i, form softmax_τ((log π_i + g_i)/τ), and anneal τ → 0 to recover discrete argmax. The result is a differentiable approximation to one-hot samples. Crucially, this is a *reparameterization* — gradients flow through the softmax — so you can train networks with discrete latent variables by backprop. Temperature annealing is critical: high τ has low gradient variance but high bias; low τ has high variance and gives true discrete behavior.

**Peng, Thomson, Smith. "Backpropagating through Structured Argmax using a SPIGOT." ACL 2018, pp. 1863–1873 (arXiv:1805.04658).** SPIGOT is a straight-through-style trick for backpropagating through a *hard structured decision* (e.g., parsing) that sits in the middle of a network. It defines a surrogate gradient that, after a parameter update, projects the intermediate structure to remain well-formed. The companion **Martins & Niculae, "Notes on Latent Structure Models and SPIGOT" (arXiv:1907.10348, 2019)** gives a clean optimization-theory interpretation linking SPIGOT to straight-through estimators and structured projections.

**Which is most applicable to temporal sequence label structures?** For your problem (per-slot activity + binary AT_HOME, with temporal persistence and exclusivity constraints), the answer is unambiguous: **the linear-chain CRF with exact forward-backward**. It is exact, cheap, gives you the partition function for free, and supports both training (NLL) and inference (Viterbi). Gumbel-softmax is the right tool if you also need *discrete latent* variables (e.g., a latent occupancy regime); SparseMAP is overkill unless you need to model assignments/matchings; Perturb-and-MAP and SPIGOT are needed only if you have a downstream non-differentiable structured decoder. For your *output* problem, CRF beats all of them.

### 4. Self-Supervised Pretraining as Structural Prior

**Devlin, Chang, Lee, Toutanova. "BERT: Pre-training of Deep Bidirectional Transformers for Language Understanding." NAACL-HLT 2019, pp. 4171–4186.** The masked language modeling (MLM) objective selects 15% of the tokens for prediction; of those, 80% are replaced with the special [MASK] token, 10% are replaced with a random token from the vocabulary, and 10% are left unchanged — the latter two preventing train/inference mismatch. The network is trained to recover the masked tokens conditional on the unmasked context. Although the MLM loss is pointwise (cross-entropy on each masked position independently), the *conditioning* context contains the surrounding tokens, so the predictor at position *t* must integrate joint information — the model implicitly learns conditional distributions p(y_t | y_{−t}). Theoretical analyses (Wang & Cho 2019; later extensions) argue MLM amounts to training a *pseudo-likelihood* of the joint distribution, which is a consistent (though not maximum-likelihood) estimator of joints.

**Raffel, Shazeer, Roberts, Lee, Narang, Matena, Zhou, Li, Liu. "Exploring the Limits of Transfer Learning with a Unified Text-to-Text Transformer." *Journal of Machine Learning Research* 21(140): 1–67, 2020 (arXiv:1910.10683).** T5 generalizes MLM to *span corruption* — mask contiguous spans, predict them sequence-to-sequence. This is closer to your "masked-diary modeling" idea: predict held-out time-slot spans from surrounding context. The seq2seq decoder is autoregressive, so unlike pure MLM it *does* model the joint over the masked span explicitly via p(y_mask | y_context) = Π_i p(y_{mask, i} | y_{mask, <i}, y_context).

**The argument for masked-diary pretraining.** If you pretrain by masking contiguous blocks of time slots and reconstructing them autoregressively (T5-style) or via discrete diffusion, the model is *forced* to model joint coherence among the masked variables. This biases its representations toward joint structure even before fine-tuning. Empirically across NLP this works: BERT/T5 representations transfer to downstream structured tasks (NER, parsing) with modest fine-tuning.

**Does the structural prior survive pointwise fine-tuning?** The honest answer from the NLP literature is **partially**. Fine-tuning with pointwise CE preserves much of the encoder's joint capability for short-range structure (adjacent-label consistency) but is empirically known to degrade long-range and rare-structure performance. Catastrophic forgetting of structure is real; mitigations include lower learning rates, layerwise decay, and structured fine-tuning losses. For your problem, expect masked-diary pretraining to *help* with all three failure modes but to *not fully solve* any of them — especially temporal persistence, which is a long-range property.

**Contrastive sequence learning.** The user asked specifically about "SeqCLR." The publication here is **Mohsenvand, Izadi & Maes, "Contrastive Representation Learning for Electroencephalogram Classification," *Proceedings of Machine Learning for Health (NeurIPS 2020 Workshop)*, PMLR 136: 238–253**. It adapts SimCLR to EEG time series via channel recombination and a contrastive loss on time-series augmentations. For *general* time-series contrastive learning, the more canonical references are:
- **Franceschi, Dieuleveut, Jaggi. "Unsupervised Scalable Representation Learning for Multivariate Time Series." NeurIPS 2019 (arXiv:1901.10738)** — triplet loss with time-based positive sampling.
- **Eldele et al. "Time-Series Representation Learning via Temporal and Contextual Contrasting" (TS-TCC), IJCAI 2021 (arXiv:2106.14112)**.
- **Yue et al. TS2Vec, AAAI 2022**.
- **Tonekaboni, Eytan, Goldenberg. "Unsupervised Representation Learning for Time Series with Temporal Neighborhood Coding" (TNC), ICLR 2021**.

None of these directly addresses joint coherence; they are good representation learners, but the structural prior they instill is general-purpose, not constraint-specific.

**Empirical bottom line on pretraining as structural prior.** Pretraining helps but does not substitute for an explicit structured output layer when constraints are hard. The fine-tuning objective matters: if it is pointwise, the prior degrades; if it is structured (CRF NLL, sequence-level), the prior is preserved.

### 5. Auxiliary Structural Losses as Pragmatic Middle Ground

Auxiliary structured-flavor losses are appealing because they require no architectural changes: just add a regularizer like
  L_aux = λ · Σ_t penalty(y_t, y_{t+1}, AT_HOME_t)
to the main pointwise loss. Examples:
- **Pairwise exclusivity**: λ · Σ_t Σ_{a,b ∈ forbidden} p(y_t = a) · p(AT_HOME_t = b).
- **Transition penalty**: λ · Σ_t KL(p(y_t) || p(y_{t-1})) or λ · Σ_t 𝟙[y_t ≠ y_{t-1}] to encourage persistence.
- **Co-occurrence regularizer**: penalize the divergence between the model's marginal joint co-occurrence and empirical co-occurrence.

**When they help.** They are reasonably effective when (a) constraints are *soft* (preferences, not hard rules), (b) λ is small enough to act as a gentle prior rather than as an objective, and (c) the constraint is well-aligned with the primary loss (e.g., transition smoothness for a primary loss that is already coarsely correct).

**When they hurt.** They cause **gradient conflict** when the auxiliary loss's gradient direction is opposed to the primary loss's. This is the classical multi-task pathology and the literature is unambiguous: large λ on a conflicting auxiliary will collapse a head, exactly as you observed. The mechanism: if the auxiliary loss can be trivially minimized by setting a head's output to a degenerate value (e.g., always predicting the majority class), and the gradient toward that degenerate point is large, the head will collapse there because the primary loss alone cannot escape the local minimum.

**Multi-task perspective.**
- **Kendall, Gal, Cipolla. "Multi-Task Learning Using Uncertainty to Weigh Losses for Scene Geometry and Semantics." CVPR 2018, pp. 7482–7491 (arXiv:1705.07115).** Treats each task loss as the log-likelihood under a Gaussian with task-specific homoscedastic variance σ_i²; the network *learns* both σ_i and the predictor. This automatically down-weights tasks with high inherent uncertainty (the auxiliary structural penalty often has higher inherent uncertainty than the primary classification loss, so it gets automatically attenuated).
- **Chen, Badrinarayanan, Lee, Rabinovich. "GradNorm: Gradient Normalization for Adaptive Loss Balancing in Deep Multitask Networks." ICML 2018, PMLR 80: 794–803 (arXiv:1711.02257).** Dynamically tunes per-task loss weights so per-task gradient norms (post-shared-encoder) are balanced. Has one hyperparameter (asymmetry α) and consistently beats fixed grid search.
- **PCGrad (Yu et al., NeurIPS 2020)** and **CAGrad (Liu et al., NeurIPS 2021)** project conflicting task gradients into non-conflicting subspaces — a more aggressive intervention than reweighting.

**Theoretical and empirical conditions for fight.** An auxiliary loss L_aux fights the primary L_main when ⟨∇L_main, ∇L_aux⟩ < 0 averaged over a batch. In your case, the transition penalty rewards staying in the same activity for many steps; the primary CE loss rewards matching the (potentially noisy) per-slot ground truth; if the ground truth has frequent legitimate transitions, the auxiliary literally disagrees with the data and the gradients fight. Diagnostics: log the dot products of per-loss gradients on the shared trunk during training; if they are negative for many batches, you have conflict.

**Connection to your head collapse.** The most likely mechanism in your case is one of:
1. The auxiliary loss provides a trivial-prediction shortcut (e.g., always predict the most common activity), and the primary loss alone cannot escape the basin of attraction once you weight the auxiliary too highly.
2. The auxiliary loss has much higher *gradient magnitude* than the primary; GradNorm would have caught this. Always log per-loss gradient norms during multi-objective training.
3. Label-imbalance amplification: the auxiliary "pulls" the head toward majority-class predictions when the majority class is also the most temporally persistent, compounding both issues.

**Verdict.** Auxiliary structural losses are a last resort, not a first one. They are useful for *soft* constraints and *hyperparameter exploration* but are systematically dominated by an explicit CRF layer for *hard* structural constraints (mutual exclusivity, transition structure). If you must use them, deploy uncertainty weighting or GradNorm and log gradient cosines.

### 6. Explicit CRF/HMM Layers on Top of Transformers

**Lafferty, McCallum, Pereira. "Conditional Random Fields: Probabilistic Models for Segmenting and Labeling Sequence Data." *Proceedings of the Eighteenth International Conference on Machine Learning* (ICML 2001), Morgan Kaufmann, pp. 282–289.** The original CRF paper, motivated by the observation that maximum-entropy Markov models (MEMMs) suffer from *label bias* — local renormalization at each step causes states with few successors to be favored. CRFs globally normalize and avoid this. The linear-chain CRF defines

  p(y | x) = (1/Z(x)) · exp(Σ_t [φ_unary(x, t, y_t) + ψ_pair(y_{t-1}, y_t)])

where ψ_pair is a learnable K × K transition matrix and φ_unary comes from the encoder. Forward-backward computes Z(x) and marginals in O(T·K²); Viterbi gives the MAP. The standard recipe for *Transformer + CRF*: feed Transformer hidden states through a linear head to get φ_unary, learn ψ_pair jointly, train by minimizing NLL = −log p(y* | x) = −Σ_t φ_unary(...) − Σ_t ψ_pair(...) + log Z(x).

**Linear-chain CRF + Transformer for NER — does it help?** Mixed evidence:
- **Souza, Nogueira, Lotufo. "Portuguese Named Entity Recognition using BERT-CRF." arXiv:1909.10649, 2019 (v2 Feb 2020).** Reports +2.55 F1 (selective NEs) and +1.41 F1 (total NEs) for adding a CRF on top of feature-based ML-BERT on HAREM I. The paper explicitly notes "the post-processing step of filtering out invalid transitions for the IOB2 scheme increases the F1-scores by 2.24 and 1.26 points, in average, for the feature-based and fine-tuning approaches, respectively." So CRF helps even in fine-tuning, but the gap shrinks under fine-tuning because BERT already learns most label dependencies.
- **Chen, Zhu, Wang. "BERT for Joint Intent Classification and Slot Filling." arXiv:1902.10909, 2019.** On ATIS: Joint BERT 97.5 / 96.1 / 88.2 (intent / slot F1 / sentence); Joint BERT+CRF 97.9 / 96.0 / 88.6. On Snips, +CRF is slightly *worse* (98.6 / 97.0 / 92.8 → 98.4 / 96.7 / 92.6). Their headline conclusion: CRF gives marginal-to-no gain when the encoder is BERT.
- **Public CoNLL-2003 reproductions**: BertForTokenClassification 92.17 F1 vs BERT+CRF 92.29 F1 — a delta of +0.12, well within noise (Louis-udm GitHub reproduction; illustrative, not peer-reviewed).

**Higher-order CRFs.** Second- and higher-order CRFs (transition on y_{t-2}, y_{t-1}, y_t) blow up Z(x) to O(T·K^{order+1}); for K = 5 activities × 2 AT_HOME states = 10 effective states, second-order is still tractable (O(T·10³) = O(1000T)) and worth trying if first-order is insufficient. The literature here is thinner because BERT-scale encoders typically already capture order-2 dependencies in their context. Notable: **Cuong, Ye, Lee, Chieu, Wu. "Conditional Random Field with High-Order Dependencies for Sequence Labeling and Segmentation." *JMLR* 15: 981–1009, 2014**.

**Semi-Markov CRFs / segmental CRFs.** **Sarawagi & Cohen. "Semi-Markov Conditional Random Fields for Information Extraction." *Advances in Neural Information Processing Systems 17* (NIPS 2004).** Semi-CRFs assign labels to *segments* (runs of consecutive y_t with the same label) rather than positions, with features that can measure segment length and segment-level properties. Cost: O(T²·K) instead of O(T·K²), with a length-cap on segments. **For your problem this is the right enhancement to a chain CRF for temporal persistence.** The neural-era reference is **Ye & Ling. "Hybrid semi-Markov CRF for Neural Sequence Labeling." ACL 2018, pp. 235–240 (arXiv:1805.03838)**, which integrates a word-level CRF and a segment-level semi-CRF and achieves SOTA on CoNLL-2003 NER without external knowledge.

**LSTM-CRF for NER (the pre-Transformer canon).** **Ma & Hovy. "End-to-end Sequence Labeling via Bi-directional LSTM-CNNs-CRF." ACL 2016, pp. 1064–1074 (arXiv:1603.01354).** Reports 97.55% accuracy on POS tagging (PTB-WSJ) and 91.21 F1 on CoNLL-2003 NER, both SOTA at the time. The architectural recipe (char-CNN + BiLSTM + CRF) was canonical until BERT. **Huang, Xu, Yu. "Bidirectional LSTM-CRF Models for Sequence Tagging." arXiv:1508.01991, 2015** is the first BiLSTM-CRF paper. **Lample, Ballesteros, Subramanian, Kawakami, Dyer. "Neural Architectures for Named Entity Recognition." NAACL 2016 (arXiv:1603.01360)** is the other major contemporary reference.

**When is a CRF redundant vs. necessary?** Heuristic from the NER literature: CRFs are *redundant* when (a) the encoder is contextual and high-capacity (BERT-scale), (b) the structural constraints are short-range and frequent in the training data (so the encoder learns them implicitly), and (c) the dataset is large. CRFs are *necessary* when (a) the encoder is weak, (b) constraints are hard combinatorial (BIO validity, exclusivity), (c) data is low-resource, and (d) you want calibrated joint probabilities (which softmax classifiers do not provide; see also JEM). Your occupancy problem is closer to "necessary" than "redundant": small-to-medium data, hard exclusivity constraint, and calibration matters for downstream simulation.

### 7. Empirical Evidence: Does Structure Help Strong Neural Baselines?

**Where structure clearly still helps post-Transformer:**
- **Low-resource NER.** Souza et al. above (HAREM I Portuguese, +1.4–3.2 F1 from CRF/transition filter, depending on regime).
- **Smart-home activity recognition.** **Thapa, Abdullah Al, Lamichhane, Yang. "A Deep Machine Learning Method for Concurrent and Interleaved Human Activity Recognition." *Sensors* 20(20): 5770, 2020 (DOI 10.3390/s20205770).** The proposed BiLSTM + Skip-Chain CRF "surpasses the previously proposed approaches with an average accuracy of more than 93%" on CASAS Kyoto, with the paper specifically arguing that "SCCRFs is a distinctive feature of conditional random field (CRF) that can represent long-term dependencies" — directly relevant to your "temporal persistence" failure mode.
- **Joint structured prediction with hard constraints.** Tasks like dependency parsing where the output is a tree; even BERT-scale models benefit from explicit decoding constraints (Eisner algorithm, MST).
- **Semi-Markov for segmentation tasks.** Ye & Ling 2018 above.

**Where neural saturates and structure does not help:**
- **CoNLL-2003 English NER with full BERT fine-tuning.** Delta from CRF ≈ 0.1 F1.
- **Joint intent / slot on ATIS, Snips.** Chen et al. above show CRF is a wash.
- **Large-scale classification benchmarks** where the encoder + softmax saturates and there is no genuine structured constraint.

**Activity recognition / time-use specifically.**
- **Lester, Choudhury, Kern, Borriello, Hannaford. "A Hybrid Discriminative/Generative Approach for Modeling Human Activities." IJCAI 2005, pp. 766–772.** Foundational paper: SVM unary + HMM temporal smoothing, 94–96% accuracy on 17 and 9 daily activities. Established that *structured smoothing on top of strong unary classifiers improves over either alone* — the canonical argument for adding a CRF/HMM to your Transformer.
- **Ordóñez & Roggen. "Deep Convolutional and LSTM Recurrent Neural Networks for Multimodal Wearable Activity Recognition." *Sensors* 16(1): 115, 2016.** DeepConvLSTM with softmax outputs (no CRF) outperforms CRF/HMM baselines on OPPORTUNITY. Counter-evidence for the "always add CRF" claim — sometimes the encoder absorbs the structure.
- **Liciotti, Bernardini, Romeo, Frontoni. "A Sequential Deep Learning Application for Recognising Human Activities in Smart Homes." *Neurocomputing* 396: 501–513, 2020.** Direct comparison of LSTM vs CRF and HMM on CASAS smart homes; LSTM beats classical CRF/HMM, supporting the view that *raw* CRF/HMM (without a neural unary) is no longer competitive — but does not directly answer whether *Transformer+CRF* > *Transformer*. Thapa et al. (2020) above is the closer answer there.

**Honest assessment of the literature.** The structured-prediction-as-output-layer literature has *not* died post-Transformer; it has stratified:
- For **large, clean, English NLP benchmarks**: pure Transformer wins or ties; CRF is a wash.
- For **low-resource, constraint-heavy, or out-of-NLP domains** (biomedical NER, smart-home HAR, occupancy modeling): structured layers still provide measurable, often statistically significant gains (1–3 absolute F1 / accuracy points).
- For **calibration / OOD / uncertainty quantification**: EBM-style joint training (JEM) provides documented benefits beyond accuracy.

The literature on the *latent*-structured-prediction frontier (SparseMAP, SPIGOT, Gumbel-softmax for discrete latents) is much more vibrant post-Transformer than the output-structured literature, because that is where the open problem is.

## Recommendations

Given "good marginals, broken joints" on a Transformer-based occupancy/activity model, here is the staged plan, ordered from highest-leverage / easiest to lower-leverage / harder:

**Stage 1 (do this first — days, not weeks).**
1. **Collapse the activity and AT_HOME heads into a single joint head.** Define a joint vocabulary V' = activities × {AT_HOME ∈ {0,1}}, but *exclude impossible combinations from V' by construction*. This eliminates mutual exclusivity violations and (activity, AT_HOME) incoherence by definition — there is no penalty to fight, just no impossible-combination logits to predict. This is the single highest-leverage intervention you can make.
2. **Diagnose**: re-measure your three failure modes (mutual exclusivity, joint coherence, temporal persistence). Modes 1 and 2 should drop to zero. Mode 3 (persistence) will likely improve modestly because the joint vocabulary is more informative, but is unlikely to fully resolve.

**Stage 2 (1–2 weeks).**
3. **Add a linear-chain CRF layer.** Treat the Transformer output as φ_unary; learn a |V'| × |V'| transition matrix ψ_pair; train with NLL (forward-backward gives exact log-Z); decode with Viterbi. Use a standard library — `pytorch-crf` or `torchcrf` are fine, or write 30 lines from scratch with `torch.logsumexp`.
4. **Benchmark Stage 2 against Stage 1** on temporal persistence specifically. Expect 1–3 absolute points of improvement, with most of the gain on rare transitions.

**Stage 3 (2–4 weeks, only if Stage 2 is insufficient).**
5. **Semi-Markov CRF.** Replace the linear-chain CRF with a semi-Markov CRF (Sarawagi & Cohen 2004; Ye & Ling 2018) with a maximum segment length L (set L to e.g. 24 slots = 6 hours, tuned to your typical activity bout). This explicitly models segment durations and should fix any residual temporal persistence pathology by penalizing segments shorter than typical activity bouts.
6. **Masked-diary pretraining.** Pretrain the Transformer encoder by masking contiguous 1–4 hour spans and reconstructing them seq2seq-style (T5 / BART objective). Then fine-tune the encoder + semi-CRF head. Expect this to help especially with rare activity types and long-range coherence.

**Stage 4 (only if you have evidence Stages 1–3 fall short).**
7. **JEM-style joint training.** Add a low-weight log p(x) term (Grathwohl et al. 2020) trained with SGLD to push the joint distribution to better-cover real data manifolds. Expect calibration and OOD gains; accuracy gains are marginal.
8. **SPEN with inference networks** (Tu & Gimpel 2018) if you have constraints that do not decompose into a chain. For pure temporal sequence labels, this is overkill.

**Auxiliary losses: only as a sanity check, not a solution.** If a stakeholder insists on a pairwise-exclusivity or transition-penalty regularizer, gate it behind Kendall–Gal–Cipolla uncertainty weighting or GradNorm. Log per-loss gradient norms and the cosine similarity of per-loss gradients each epoch; if cosine is negative, kill the auxiliary.

### Thresholds that would change the recommendation
- If Stage 1 alone resolves all three failure modes within tolerance, **stop** — do not add a CRF. The structural constraint is now baked into the output space.
- If Stage 2 improvement is < 0.5 absolute F1 / accuracy points, the CRF is not worth the complexity; instead pretrain (Stage 3.6) and refine the joint vocabulary.
- If you observe that Viterbi decoding *underperforms* greedy per-slot argmax after CRF training, your transition matrix is mis-calibrated — reduce its parameter count or add label smoothing on transitions.
- If you have < 5k training sequences, do not bother with JEM/SPEN; the variance dominates the gain.
- If downstream use requires *sampling* joint trajectories (e.g., for building-energy Monte Carlo simulation), you must train a model that exposes a proper joint distribution — a CRF (which gives p(y|x) exactly) suffices; a softmax classifier never will.

### Six papers to read in sequence

1. **Lafferty, McCallum, Pereira. "Conditional Random Fields: Probabilistic Models for Segmenting and Labeling Sequence Data." ICML 2001, pp. 282–289.** — The foundation. Read for the label-bias argument and the linear-chain factorization.
2. **LeCun, Chopra, Hadsell, Ranzato, Huang. "A Tutorial on Energy-Based Learning." In *Predicting Structured Data*, MIT Press, 2006.** — The unifying view. Read sections 1–4 for the framework and the loss-function taxonomy.
3. **Ma & Hovy. "End-to-end Sequence Labeling via Bi-directional LSTM-CNNs-CRF." ACL 2016, pp. 1064–1074 (arXiv:1603.01354).** — The canonical neural+CRF recipe. Read for the practical architecture you will adapt.
4. **Belanger & McCallum. "Structured Prediction Energy Networks." ICML 2016, PMLR 48: 983–992 (arXiv:1511.06350).** — The neural EBM. Read for the gradient-descent-as-inference idea.
5. **Grathwohl, Wang, Jacobsen, Duvenaud, Norouzi, Swersky. "Your Classifier Is Secretly an Energy Based Model and You Should Treat It Like One." ICLR 2020 (arXiv:1912.03263).** — The modern joint-distribution argument. Read for *why* training the joint pays off even when you only care about the conditional.
6. **Niculae, Corro, Nangia, Mihaylova, Martins. "Discrete Latent Structure in Neural Networks." *Foundations and Trends in Signal Processing* 19(2): 99–211, 2025; arXiv:2301.07473.** — The modern long-form survey. Read selectively as a reference for the gradient-estimation landscape (Gumbel-softmax, SparseMAP, SPIGOT, Perturb-and-MAP) when you eventually need any of those tools.

## Caveats

1. **Uncertain reference**: the user prompt cited "Niculae et al. 2020 'Structured Prediction with Deep Learning: A Survey.'" I could not locate a paper with that exact title and authors in 2020. The closest matches are (a) the **2025 *Foundations and Trends in Signal Processing* monograph** by Niculae, Corro, Nangia, Mihaylova & Martins (arXiv:2301.07473, first version Jan 2023), (b) the **ACL 2019 tutorial** by Martins, Mihaylova, Nangia & Niculae ("Latent Structure Models for Natural Language Processing," in *Proceedings of the 57th Annual Meeting of the ACL: Tutorial Abstracts*, pp. 1–5), and (c) Chauhan et al., "Structured Prediction in NLP — A Survey," arXiv:2110.02057, 2021. I have used the 2025 monograph as the canonical long-form survey in this report. If the user has a specific 2020 reference in mind, it may be a workshop preprint I missed.

2. **"SeqCLR" disambiguation**: the term has been used by two unrelated groups: (a) **Mohsenvand, Izadi & Maes (MIT Media Lab), "Contrastive Representation Learning for Electroencephalogram Classification," PMLR 136 (NeurIPS 2020 ML4H Workshop), pp. 238–253**, on EEG channels; and (b) a separately named "SeqCLR" for text recognition. The user's prompt mentioned "Franceschi or similar"; the relevant Franceschi paper is **Franceschi, Dieuleveut, Jaggi, "Unsupervised Scalable Representation Learning for Multivariate Time Series," NeurIPS 2019**, which uses triplet loss, not InfoNCE/SimCLR. There is no single canonical "SeqCLR for time-series" paper.

3. **CoNLL BERT+CRF delta**: the +0.12 F1 number is from a public GitHub reproduction (Louis-udm/NER-BERT-CRF), not a peer-reviewed paper. The peer-reviewed evidence on CoNLL-2003 typically does not directly ablate +CRF on BERT in head-to-head form; it is more common to see the ablation on languages other than English (Portuguese above) or on lower-resource splits. Treat the 0.12 number as illustrative.

4. **Empirical generalization across domains**: most of the "does CRF help" evidence I cite is NER- and HAR-specific. Your problem (per-slot building occupancy + activity) is closer to HAR than NER. The transferable lessons are (a) CRF helps more when constraints are hard and data is limited, (b) semi-CRF helps when segment-duration structure matters, and (c) the gain is typically 1–3 absolute points, not 10. Calibrate your expectations accordingly. Note that Liciotti et al. (2020) found that pure LSTM *outperformed* CRF/HMM on the same CASAS data Thapa et al. (2020) used — but Liciotti et al. compared LSTM-only vs CRF-only, whereas Thapa et al. compared BiLSTM+SCCRF vs BiLSTM+CRF. Neural+CRF beating neural-only is the relevant comparison for your decision.

5. **Architectural caveat about "head collapse"**: there are at least three mechanisms by which auxiliary losses collapse heads — trivial-shortcut minimization, gradient-magnitude domination, and label-imbalance amplification. Without seeing your code, I cannot say which one bit you. Diagnostic order: (i) check per-loss gradient norms during training, (ii) check the cosine of per-loss gradients, (iii) check whether the collapsed head's outputs match the majority-class distribution of the auxiliary's target.

6. **Decision-theoretic point**: if the *purpose* of your model is to generate joint samples (e.g., for downstream building-energy simulation), then matching marginals is not enough by definition, and the structural intervention is non-optional. If the purpose is just to predict per-slot labels for monitoring, matching marginals may actually suffice, and you should question whether the joint matters operationally before investing in Stages 2–4.

7. **Speculative-tense flag**: several papers I cite (notably the JEM and SPEN follow-up literature) state their improvements in terms of "improves calibration / robustness / OOD detection" as headline benefits. These are *measured* (with named metrics — ECE, AUROC) in the JEM paper on CIFAR-10/100 and SVHN, but in your domain (occupancy time series) the same machinery has not been directly evaluated. You should treat the JEM benefits as plausible but not yet domain-validated for your setting.
