# RT45. Prior work assessment on occupancy-aware building energy surrogates

## Section A. Direct answer

The core claim of brief section 4 - a surrogate model of building simulation whose input is a time-use occupancy sequence, trained on a paired campaign where only occupancy varies, and scored on the occupancy effect with an occupancy-blind control - is unclaimed in the published record and remains fully open. However, the wider context of hourly residential surrogates at district or stock scale (part P5) is already established and growing rapidly (C10, C11, C12). Recent surrogate models such as CityTFT (C4) and BiLSTM-UBEM (C5) predict hourly residential and commercial building energy from weather time series and archetype parameters, but they uniformly hold occupancy schedules constant using standard deterministic profiles. In parallel, time-use diaries (part P4) have been reviewed extensively (C9) but are used solely to drive stochastic schedule generators feeding forward into physics simulation software like EnergyPlus (C1), never as direct sequential inputs to a learned surrogate (part P1). Furthermore, recent work on retrofit surrogate models by Park and Park (C6) directly proves that surrogates with low error on absolute loads can completely fail to predict paired differences (causal effect of an intervention), which directly validates our proposed two-tier scoring on paired occupancy differences (part P2). Finally, while feature ablation is common in feature selection (C8), an explicit occupancy-blind negative control (part P3) to confirm that the surrogate actually uses occupant sequence data is entirely novel in the building simulation literature. The paper design therefore stands intact, but the manuscript must frame its novelty around behavioral sequence sensitivity and causal difference scoring rather than claiming a general stock-scale surrogate.

### Summary statement on the whole claim

The aspect of our claim best supported as completely new is the combination of a time-use occupancy sequence feeding a learned surrogate model with an explicit occupancy-blind negative control; the aspect already covered by published work is the high-resolution hourly surrogate modeling of residential stocks, which is already established by several 2024-2025 papers.

## Section E. What this changes in our planning

* Drop any phrasing claiming the first hourly residential building energy surrogate or the first stock-scale EnergyPlus surrogate, because C4 (Dai et al., 2025), C5 (Pan et al., 2024), and C10 (Govindarajan et al., 2025) have already established hourly district and urban building energy surrogates.
* Frame the primary scientific contribution around behavioral sequence sensitivity and causal difference scoring, using the findings of C6 (Park and Park, 2023) to argue that standard calibration metrics (RMSE, CV(RMSE)) are fundamentally insufficient to verify whether a surrogate model learns true occupant effects.
* Retain the paired simulation campaign design (varying only occupancy while holding building geometry and weather fixed), because existing stock surrogates (C4, C5, C10) vary building morphology and weather while keeping occupancy schedules static.
* Formalize the occupancy-blind control (shuffling occupancy sequences across runs) as a mandatory negative control, highlighting that standard surrogate studies (C3, C7, C8) perform at most parameter ablation or feature selection, but never verify whether sequence-dependent effects collapse under input permutation.
* Position the diary-to-surrogate pipeline directly against the state of the art identified in review C9 (Vosoughkhosravi et al., 2023) and benchmark C1 (He et al., 2015), showing that while prior work used time-use diaries to generate schedules for slow physics engines, this work uses diary sequences directly to train an instantaneous surrogate for stock analysis.

## Section G. Contradictions, gaps, open questions, and your own negative controls

### Disagreements and gaps in prior literature

* Absolute load accuracy versus causal difference fidelity: Conventional surrogate literature (e.g., Westermann and Evins C7, Govindarajan et al. C10) evaluates surrogates almost exclusively on point prediction metrics such as RMSE, MAE, and CV(RMSE). In contrast, Park and Park (C6) demonstrate that surrogate models with excellent RMSE can fail catastrophically when predicting the differential impact (savings) of an intervention. We adopt Park and Park's stance: absolute load error is an insufficient validation metric for surrogate models intended to evaluate interventions or behavioral differences.
* Occupancy representation in building energy surrogates: Urban building energy surrogate models (C4, C5, C10) claim to address stock-level energy dynamics, yet they treat occupant schedules as static, uniform profiles. This creates a severe mismatch between model capabilities and real-world peak load diversity. Our design bridges this gap by directly admitting dynamic occupancy sequences.
* Unreachable host for K3: As flagged in the prompt, direct TCP connection to the OSTI purl server (`https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/1817464`) timed out with WinError 10060. However, the complete bibliographic record, abstract, and detailed technical parameters were retrieved and verified from the official landing page (`https://www.osti.gov/biblio/1817464`).

3. Which of the candidate angles named in the prompt did you conclude are already taken?
   Angle P5 (hourly residential surrogates at stock or district scale) is already taken by Govindarajan et al. (2025), Dai et al. (2025), and Pan et al. (2024). Angle P2 (paired difference scoring) is taken in principle in the building retrofit domain by Park and Park (2023), although not for occupancy differences. The reason angles P1, P3, and P4 remain untouched is that building simulation research has historically maintained a strict division between behavioral modeling (generating occupancy schedules via Markov chains or agent models for physics engines) and surrogate modeling (emulating physics engines under varying building geometry and weather while treating schedules as static boundary conditions).
