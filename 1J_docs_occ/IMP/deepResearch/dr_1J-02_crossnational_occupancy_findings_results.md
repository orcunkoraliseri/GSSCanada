## 2. Agreement and Disagreement Per Theme

### T1. Total time at home
- Where countries agree: Both the United States (Sekar et al., 2018) and the United Kingdom (Anderson and Torriti, 2018) document long-term secular growth in time spent on in-home electronic entertainment and telework from the 1990s through the 2010s, reducing travel time and non-residential building presence.
- Where countries disagree: While the United States saw a steady rise in total time spent at home (+8 days/year from 2003 to 2012 in Sekar et al., 2018), Japan (Yin et al., 2024) documented a sharp contraction in total time allocated to primary home activities (sleep, housework, and home-based work) by 2021 across all demographic segments.

### T2. Sleep duration
- Where countries agree: Western countries (US ATUS and UK studies) report relatively stable or slightly increasing long-term sleep durations, typically averaging 8.4 to 8.8 hours per day among adults, with modest increases during remote-work periods.
- Where countries disagree: Japan (Yin et al., 2024) shows an unprecedented collapse in nighttime sleep in the 2021 STULA wave across all age groups (midnight sleep probability dropped from ~80% to below 60%), directly contrasting with the stable or elevated sleep durations recorded in the US ATUS.

### T3. Weekday versus weekend time at home
- Where countries agree: Across all surveyed countries prior to 2020 (US, UK, Australia, Denmark), weekend daytime presence is substantially higher than weekday presence (typically by 15 to 25 percentage points between 09:00 and 17:00, or a 2.0 to 3.5 hour presence gap).
- Where countries disagree: In the post-2020 period, US studies (Dong et al., 2024) find that teleworking weekdays converged toward weekend load shapes, whereas studies with low telework penetration maintained a sharp weekday-weekend disparity.

### T4. Household size and occupancy
- Where countries agree: When household occupancy is defined as "at least one occupant present", all international studies (Richardson 2008 in the UK, Jeong 2021 in Australia, Koupaei 2022 in the US) find that larger households exhibit substantially MORE occupied hours and far lower daytime vacancy than single-person homes.
- Where countries disagree: No international study finds an inverse relationship between household size and "at least one member home" occupancy hours; any inverse pattern reported in individual cohorts arises from per-capita presence accounting or dwelling-type filtering rather than multi-person pooling.

### T5. Timing of in-home activity and heat gains
- Where countries agree: All international building performance studies (Mitra 2020 in the US, Buttitta 2020 in the UK, Aerts 2014 in Belgium) agree that static code schedules (e.g. ASHRAE 90.1, Building America, national defaults) misplace the diurnal timing and shape of internal heat gains (by up to 2 to 4 hours) rather than simply underestimating total annual presence.
- Where countries disagree: The specific timing shift varies by climate and national lifestyle: US studies report earlier morning ramp-ups and later evening cooling peaks, whereas Southern European studies (e.g. HETUS Italy/Spain) show pronounced mid-afternoon lunch presence plateaus absent in North American data.

### T6. The COVID-19 break
- Where countries agree: All countries that collected time-use microdata during 2020 to 2023 (US ATUS, UK ONS, Japan STULA) observe a major structural break from pre-2020 baselines, marked by sharp drops in transit and office presence, and persistent increases in weekday work-from-home share (stabilizing around 30% to 35% in the US and UK in 2022 to 2023).
- Where countries disagree: In the US and UK, the COVID break produced a net expansion of daytime in-home presence; in Japan (STULA 2021), the pandemic coincided with shortened work hours and nocturnal routine disruption rather than daytime in-home occupancy expansion.
