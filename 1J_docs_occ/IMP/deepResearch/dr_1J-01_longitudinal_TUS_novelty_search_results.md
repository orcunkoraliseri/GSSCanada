## 5. The Three Closest Studies

### 1. Chen et al. (2022), Applied Energy 325, 119890
Chen et al. (2022) is the closest precedent in occupant schedule generation for building energy modeling. It scores Y on three columns: C1 (American Time Use Survey microdata), C3 (aggregates individual occupant presence into household-level occupancy schedules), and C6 (coupled directly to NREL ResStock and EnergyPlus across 550,000 dwelling models representing the contiguous U.S. housing stock). Quoted: "This stochastic simulator is integrated with ResStock, a large-scale residential building stock simulation tool, to demonstrate the capability of stochastic residential building load modeling at scale" (Abstract, p. 1). It scores PARTLY on C2 because while it pools five years of ATUS microdata (2014 to 2018) to ensure sample size, it treats them as a single static cross-sectional pool rather than analyzing longitudinal shifts across separate survey cycles. It cleanly misses C4 and C5: the model is strictly retrospective, contains no generative projection to a future year past the survey, and provides no out-of-sample quantitative validation. Quoted: "This study demonstrates baseline retrospective capability... future work is needed to explore future demographic and occupancy changes" (Section 5, p. 18).

### 2. Yin, Yamaguchi, Zajch, Uchida and Shimoda (2024), ASim 2024 proceedings, 1321 to 1328
Yin et al. (2024) is the closest precedent in multi-cycle longitudinal time-use analysis for residential energy demand. It scores Y on two columns: C1 (Japanese Survey on Time Use and Leisure Activities Diary A microdata) and C2 (five distinct survey cycles spanning 20 years: 2001, 2006, 2011, 2016, and 2021). Quoted: "This study used Japanese time use survey data from 2001 to 2021 to analyze long-term temporal changes in activity patterns" (Abstract, p. 1321). It misses C3 because it segments respondents into six individual demographic categories rather than assembling combined multi-member household presence schedules. It misses C4 and C5 because it analyzes historical changes across observed survey cycles and does not project or generate future populations past 2021. Quoted: "Future research can combine with machine learning methods and synthetic populations to explore how shifts in activity patterns influence energy demand for urban-scale regions" (Abstract, p. 1321; Section 5, p. 1328). It misses C6 because it stops at econometric logistic regression of activity probabilities and does not execute physical building performance simulations.

### 3. Sekar, Williams and Chen (2018), Joule 2, 521 to 536
Sekar et al. (2018) is the most prominent multi-cycle time-use energy study in the literature. It scores Y on two columns: C1 (American Time Use Survey microdata) and C2 (ten annual survey waves from 2003 to 2012 analyzed in one consistent framework). Quoted: "We analyze American Time Use Survey (ATUS) data from 2003 to 2012 to quantify changes in time spent on various activities and their effect on energy consumption" (Summary, p. 521). It misses C3 because ATUS interviews only one person per household and the study evaluates individual time allocation rather than assembled whole-household schedules. It misses C4 and C5 because it is a retrospective accounting of past changes that does not project beyond 2012. It scores PARTLY on C6 because it maps time-use changes to national energy use via sector-level energy-intensity multipliers rather than running dynamic physical building performance simulations.
