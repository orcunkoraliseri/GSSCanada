# RT19: Field Map of Occupancy Data Sources Beyond National Time-Use Surveys

## Section A. Direct answer

The literature using non-survey open data to derive building occupancy schedules for energy modelling is sparse, fragmented, and dominated by single-building sensing. Among the eleven surveyed source families, only smart thermostats (Family 1) and smart-meter load disaggregation (Family 3) have achieved a sustained volume of residential presence studies, though both suffer from acute selection bias and proprietary data silos. Cellular mobility data (Family 5), household travel surveys (Family 7), and crowdsourced web check-ins (Family 10) have successfully derived urban-scale occupancy for commercial and mixed-use buildings, but almost completely lack dwelling-level residential resolution. Day-night population grids (Family 6) and distribution-feeder load signals (Family 4) remain virtually untouched as direct inputs to building energy simulation, functioning at best as coarse regional boundary checks. National time-use surveys (Family 11, 64 works from 2015 to 2026, 100 % true-positive share) remain a small academic niche in absolute counts, but they represent the only source family providing demographically resolved, multi-activity domestic presence schedules with zero commercial hardware bias.

## Section E. What this changes in our planning

* **Drop day-night population grids (Family 6) as an occupancy generator for UBEM.** With zero true positives in the building energy literature (Table B1, row F06) and a 1 km or 250 m raster resolution that collapses residential and commercial footprints, population grids cannot inform dwelling-level presence. They can only serve as an upper-bound spatial headcount check (`R2`).
* **Treat smart thermostat telemetry (Family 1) strictly as an external validation benchmark (`R3`), never as an unweighted donor pool.** Smart thermostats show strong uptake in building energy research (79 papers, row F01), but suffer from severe demographic skew: single-family suburban homeowners with higher incomes dominate ecobee and Nest user bases, lacking representation for low-income multi-unit tenants.
* **Combine household travel surveys (Family 7) with time-use diaries to ground commute and out-of-home durations.** Travel surveys exhibit 100 % true-positive precision in deriving building presence (Table B1, row F07; Table C2, rows M01-M03). They offer local metropolitan granularity (e.g. Montreal Origin-Destination survey, Toronto TTS) that national time-use surveys lack.
* **Reject crowdsourced web data (Family 10) for residential modelling.** Social media check-ins and POI opening hours derive valid occupancy schedules for restaurants, retail, and hotels (row F10, row M05), but provide zero signal for domestic residential life.
* **Frame national time-use surveys (Family 11) as an indispensable demographic anchor, not an obsolete source.** While smart meters and thermostats capture presence without activity, only time-use microdata capture who is home, what appliance they are operating, and with whom. The research gap sits in calibrating these diaries against empirical non-survey signals without inducing circularity.

## Section G. Contradictions, gaps, open questions, and negative controls

### Contradictions and gaps identified across sources

* **Indoor phone apps versus macro cellular mobility (Family 5).** OpenAlex queries for `"mobile phone" occupancy building` overwhelmingly returned papers on local indoor smartphone apps communicating via Bluetooth Low Energy (BLE) or Wi-Fi beacons (4 of 5 top hits, 80 % false positives for urban mobility). Macro mobility studies (such as Barbour et al. 2019) represent a distinct and very small literature, requiring explicit queries with terms like `mobility` or `"cellular data"`.
* **Day-night population grids are completely disconnected from BEM (Family 6).** Despite widespread adoption in disaster management and spatial epidemiology (LandScan, ENACT-POP), zero studies in the top hits used population grids to drive or calibrate building energy simulations. Grid rasters mix residential, office, and transit populations indiscriminately, making them uninformative for dwelling-level thermal models.
* **Smart meter presence inference accuracy drops during baseload periods (Family 3).** Virtual occupancy sensing literature (Jin et al. 2017, Razavi et al. 2019) claims 80 % to 90 % accuracy during active hours, but acknowledges failure during sleeping hours and quiet daytime periods where vampire power from appliances obscures human absence.
* **The crowdsourcing blindspot for residential occupancy (Family 10).** Check-ins and footfall counts provide high-fidelity hourly curves for commercial dining, retail, and hotels, but contain zero signal for domestic living. Fusing crowdsourced data with UBEM works only for non-residential channels.
