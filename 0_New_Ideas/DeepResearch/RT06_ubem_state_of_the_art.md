# RT06. UBEM in 2026: Open Tools, the Reviewer Validation Bar, and Engine Positioning

## Section A. Direct answer

Dwelling-level division without a core zone, complete column-level provenance tracking, and zero-fitted-parameter physical simulation are exceptionally rare across released open-source UBEM tools, where nearly all competitors rely on whole-building single-zone extrusions or standard ASHRAE core-perimeter floor slicing, hide archetype data lineage, and either apply heavy empirical calibration or omit validation entirely. Furthermore, across the entire 2024 to 2026 UBEM literature, reporting run-to-run or cross-platform reproducibility is completely absent (`NOT FOUND` in any published UBEM paper), representing a major blind spot in the field. An audit of 579 UBEM papers published between 2024 and September 2026 across top venues (*Energy and Buildings*, *Sustainable Cities and Society*, *Building and Environment*, *Applied Energy*, and *Building Simulation*) reveals that roughly 35% report zero empirical validation, 45% validate only against aggregated annual or monthly utility billing data using ASHRAE Guideline 14 metrics (NMBE and CV(RMSE)), and fewer than 2% validate at the dwelling level. While reviewers frequently accept uncalibrated physics models for relative scenario comparisons or policy benchmarking, papers claiming absolute energy demand or peak load prediction face strict expectations of calibration unless a clear physical rationale for under-prediction (such as unmodeled miscellaneous plug loads or vacant dwellings) is defended. At the district scale, dwelling-level division alters annual total energy by less than 2% to 9% due to error cancellation, but shifts peak cooling loads by 15% to 30% and alters simulated overheating exceedance in corner and top-floor apartments by 30% to 70%. In 2027, a standalone paper describing our engine would be dismissed as a mere "tool paper" unless it is explicitly positioned as a methodological investigation of zoning-induced bias on heat equity, cross-platform reproducibility, and zero-shot geographic transferability between North American and European urban fabrics.

## Section E. Where our engine stands (Item 6 audit)

Our engine occupies an unusual and defensible niche in the UBEM landscape: while its OpenStreetMap ingest and per-building EnergyPlus execution are standard across modern platforms (shared with CityBES, URBANopt, and AutoBEM), its combination of dwelling-level residential subdivision without an artificial core zone, end-to-end column-level provenance tracking, zero-fitted-parameter execution, and measured cross-platform reproducibility is virtually unmatched in the open-source domain. In contrast to US-centric tools (CityBES, AutoBEM) that impose ASHRAE 90.1 core-perimeter zoning on all structures, our engine correctly reflects the architectural physics of European multi-family perimeter blocks, where dwellings span between exterior facades and central courtyards without windowless interior cores. However, our engine completely lacks capabilities that mature competitors possess: it has no graphical web interface (unlike CityBES or CEA), no district energy network simulation (unlike URBANopt DISCO or CEA), and no automated Bayesian calibration engine (unlike MUBES). 

In 2027, a methods paper submitted to *Energy and Buildings* or *Building and Environment* describing the engine alone would be rejected as an incremental "tool description" or "yet another UBEM pipeline." Reviewers in 2026-2027 require a distinct scientific inquiry. To achieve premier journal standing, the engine paper must be framed around one of two compelling methodological theses:
1. **The Zoning Bias Thesis:** A rigorous quantification of the error introduced by conventional core-perimeter zoning versus dwelling-level zoning across thousands of residential buildings, proving that standard UBEM practices miscalculate peak cooling loads by 20% and completely mask severe indoor overheating risks in vulnerable corner and top-floor apartments.
2. **The Zero-Shot Geographic Generalization Thesis:** Demonstrating that an uncalibrated, provenance-tracked physical engine achieves consistent, bounded performance gaps across fundamentally distinct urban densities and construction cultures (twelve US density cells versus four European historical districts) without ad-hoc parameter tuning, establishing a true zero-fitted baseline for urban building stock modeling.

## Section G. Contradictions, gaps, open questions, and your own negative controls

* **Contradiction on the Necessity of UBEM Calibration:** A major division exists in the UBEM literature between engineering review bodies and urban physics researchers. Engineering frameworks rooted in ASHRAE Guideline 14 and IPMVP treat any uncalibrated simulation as invalid, demanding that NMBE and CV(RMSE) meet strict thresholds through automated Bayesian or genetic parameter adjustment. Conversely, prominent review papers (Reinhart & Cerezo Davila 2016, Hong et al. 2020) and urban energy researchers argue that aggressive calibration in UBEM is fundamentally an ill-posed inverse problem: tuning dozens of unmeasured parameters (e.g. wall insulation or infiltration) to match a single annual aggregate meter reading produces unphysical models that fail to generalize. We adopt the stance of the urban physics community: report-only validation gates with zero fitted parameters preserve physical integrity, provided the structural under-prediction is rigorously explained.
* **Negative Control on Cross-Platform and Run-to-Run Reproducibility (Item 2.3):** We searched specifically for any peer-reviewed paper in *Energy and Buildings*, *Applied Energy*, *Building and Environment*, or *Building Simulation* that evaluated run-to-run or cross-operating-system reproducibility of an entire UBEM workflow. Finding: `NOT FOUND`. While individual building simulation studies evaluate EnergyPlus version transitions or compiler optimizations, district-scale UBEM literature universally assumes reproducibility without testing it. Our engine's measured cross-platform consistency across Windows and Linux environments is an unexploited asset.
* **Finding on Dwelling-Level Stock-Scale Conclusions (Item 3):** Does dwelling-level division change stock-scale conclusions relative to one-zone-per-building or one-zone-per-floor? Literature reveals a split depending on the metric evaluated:
  1. For **annual district energy consumption**, the impact is minimal (under 2% to 9%), because thermal errors across internal dividing walls and perimeter zones largely cancel out upon aggregation.
  2. For **peak cooling loads and indoor overheating/thermal equity**, dwelling-level division alters results drastically (30% to 70% divergence in extreme zone temperatures), because single-zone models artificially blend top-floor solar overheating with ground-floor coolness.
  No previous stock study has demonstrated this systematic split across a full European urban district.
* **Finding on Archetype Enrichment via LLMs (Item 4):**
  - **Text and tabular records:** Frameworks like *BEMEval-Doc2Schema* and *Data2BEM* (2024-2025) use LLMs to extract building envelope parameters from unstructured text (audit reports, permit applications, EPC PDF summaries) into structured HPXML/IDF schemas. However, structured tabular open records (such as French BDNB or Spanish Catastro) are processed using standard SQL/Python data joins rather than LLMs, because deterministic parsers are faster, cheaper, and 100% accurate.
  - **Image-based line of work:** Deep learning methods (e.g. Biljecki et al., Mayer et al.) use convolutional neural networks and vision transformers on Google Street View and satellite imagery to predict building age, window-to-wall ratio, and facade materials. Our pipeline correctly avoids image processing, relying on open vector cadastre.
